# Conclusions and Recommendations {#sec:ch5}

## Conclusions

This research developed and evaluated an integrated MATLAB/Simulink–Python framework for diagnosing incipient power-transformer faults from measured dissolved gases. The framework produces conventional and machine-learning diagnoses from the same record and compares them statistically, and it presents both to the engineer on a simulated operating transformer. The conclusions are given objective by objective.

- **Conventional methods.** The Rogers ratio, IEC 60599 ratio and Duval Triangle 1 methods were implemented in MATLAB from documented tables and verified against the worked examples of their sources and against an independent Python implementation, which agreed on all 2 481 answers. The ratio methods left a large share of records without a diagnosis (IEC 60599 {{iec_nd_all_pct}} and Rogers {{rogers_nd_all_pct}} of {{n_master}} records), while the Duval Triangle always answered and was the most accurate conventional method on every test set.
- **Data.** A table of {{n_master}} published records with five gases was assembled with documented label origin. The audit found and removed {{n_removed_leak}} training records that leaked test or benchmark cases. It also found that one published training file contains relabelled IEC TC 10 cases, so only the {{n_iec}} inspection-confirmed IEC TC 10 power transformers were used as the main benchmark.
- **Machine learning.** Five classifiers were compared by leakage-free nested cross-validation. A random forest on 18 engineered features was selected (macro-F1 {{cv_F4_random_forest}}); it was statistically indistinguishable from XGBoost, and simple logistic regression came within 0.03. The model's explanations agree with gas-formation physics: C2H2 drives the discharge classes, hydrogen and the CH4/H2 ratio drive partial discharge, and C2H4 drives the high-temperature thermal faults.
- **Reduced gases.** Only one reduced panel kept the loss of macro-F1 within the tolerance of {{rg_delta}}: H2, C2H6, C2H4 and C2H2 (loss {{rg_min_loss}} {{rg_min_loss_ci}}). No conventional method can interpret this panel, because all of them need methane. Hydrogen alone was far from sufficient for diagnosis.
- **ML versus conventional methods.** On the internal and Li test sets the ML model was significantly more accurate than every conventional method. On the inspection-confirmed IEC TC 10 benchmark it was significantly more accurate than the Rogers method only; it did not differ significantly from IEC 60599, and the Duval Triangle had the higher point estimate ({{iec_duv_strict}} against {{iec_ml_strict}}). Disagreement between ML and the conventional methods was common, but with the approved normality gate it was not concentrated near the decision boundaries; without the gate, disagreement with the Duval Triangle did increase near its zone boundaries. The research hypothesis is therefore not supported on the main benchmark and is partly supported on the other two sets.
- **Integration.** A Simscape thermal model of a 40 MVA transformer met its verification tolerance by a wide margin (largest hot-spot difference {{sim_t2_emax_hs}} K against 0.1 K). A FastAPI service and a MATLAB App Designer dashboard were linked to it. Over all {{n_heldout}} held-out records the integrated system returned exactly the offline diagnoses (fidelity 1), with a 95th-percentile response time of {{int_p95}} ms against the 1 000 ms target.

The overall conclusion is that machine learning is a useful complement to conventional DGA interpretation, not a replacement for it. On clearly inspected faults the best conventional method is as accurate as a carefully validated ML model, but ML always gives an answer, works with gas panels that the conventional methods cannot use, and disagrees with them often enough that showing both answers together gives the engineer real information. The exploratory study suggests that a model which answers only when it is confident could be more accurate than the ratio methods at the same coverage; this needs confirmation on fresh data.

## Recommendations

The following are recommended:

- Utilities that interpret DGA should show conventional and ML diagnoses side by side, as the dashboard of this study does, and treat samples on which they disagree as candidates for closer surveillance or resampling, rather than replacing the conventional methods.
- The Duval Triangle should be kept as the reference conventional method; the Rogers ratio method should not be used alone, because it cannot diagnose low-energy discharges and leaves many samples without a diagnosis.
- When online monitors are chosen for diagnosis rather than detection, monitors that measure at least H2, C2H6, C2H4 and C2H2 should be preferred. Hydrogen-only monitors should be treated as fault detectors, not diagnostic tools.
- Researchers who report ML results for DGA should document the origin of their labels, remove duplicate and leaked records, use nested cross-validation and a measure such as macro-F1, test on inspection-confirmed cases, compare with the conventional methods by paired tests, and state the home advantage of the conventional methods on the IEC TC 10 database.
- Ghanaian utilities such as GRIDCo, ECG and VRA, through the University, should be approached for DGA records with inspection-confirmed faults; such data are needed to confirm the findings and the reject option on local transformers.

## Suggestions for Further Work

Further work could:

- validate the model and the reject option on new inspection-confirmed data, preferably from Ghanaian utilities, and extend the inputs to CO and CO2 so that faults involving paper can be identified;
- replace the default thermal constants and the illustrative scenarios of the Simulink model with the nameplate and heat-run data and the measured load profiles of a real transformer, and confirm the limits of the conventional methods against the current full editions of IEC 60599, IEEE C57.104 and IEC 60076-7;
- add trend analysis of gas generation rates and the uncertainty of laboratory measurements to the diagnosis; and
- harden the service for networked use (authentication, logging and monitoring) and test it on utility hardware.
