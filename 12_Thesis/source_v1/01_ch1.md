# General Introduction {#sec:ch1}

## Background to the Research

Reliable electricity supply underpins the economic and social development of every nation, and power transformers sit at the centre of that reliability: they change the voltage at every stage between the generating station and the consumer. A power transformer is one of the most expensive items in a substation, it takes many months to replace, and its failure can interrupt supply to large areas. International surveys carried out by working groups of the International Council on Large Electric Systems (CIGRE) show that major transformer failures are rare but costly. The working group A2.37 survey collected 964 major failures from 58 utilities in 21 countries, with failures related to windings, tap changers and bushings as the main contributors (Tenbohlen, Jagers and Vahidi, 2017), and a later analysis of CIGRE surveys covering 26 533 transformers reported an overall major failure rate of 0.41 % per year (Martin and Tenbohlen, 2026). Because failures are rare in any single utility but expensive when they occur, utilities rely on condition monitoring to find developing faults early (Murugan and Ramasamy, 2019).

Ghana's own power sector illustrates the consequences. On 23 April 2026 a technical problem on the Ghana Grid Company (GRIDCo) line at the Asiekpe bulk supply point, together with a transformer fault in the Tema region, left communities across the Volta, Oti and Tema regions without power (Anon., 2026). In 2024 the Electricity Company of Ghana (ECG) attributed evening outages to some 630 overloaded distribution transformers; the Public Utilities Regulatory Commission (PURC) examined the loading records and found that claim "not factually accurate", tracing most of the outages to load management and to faults unrelated to transformer overloading (Dotse, 2024). Both cases show that the condition of transformers, and reliable information about that condition, are live operational concerns. They bear directly on the affordable and reliable energy that Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 7 commits nations to deliver (Anon., 2015).

Dissolved Gas Analysis (DGA) has for several decades been the most widely used condition-monitoring technique for oil-immersed power transformers (Suwarno *et al.*, 2024). Thermal and electrical stresses decompose the insulating oil and the cellulose paper, and the resulting gases dissolve in the oil. Hydrogen (H2), methane (CH4), ethane (C2H6), ethylene (C2H4) and acetylene (C2H2) are formed in proportions that depend on the type and energy of the fault, so an oil sample taken from a working transformer can reveal an incipient fault long before it becomes a failure (Duval, 2002). The conventional interpretation methods built on this principle, such as the Rogers ratio method, the IEC 60599 three-ratio method and the Duval Triangle, remain in routine use by utilities. They rely on fixed limits and zone boundaries derived from historical fault populations (Rogers, 1978; Duval and dePablo, 2001; Duval, 2002).

Over the past decade, machine learning (ML) has been applied widely to DGA. Support vector machines, artificial neural networks, k-nearest neighbours, random forests and gradient-boosted trees learn the relationship between gas concentrations and confirmed faults directly from data instead of from fixed rules (Rao *et al.*, 2021; Dladla and Thango, 2025a). A systematic review of 124 studies published between 2014 and 2024 found that support vector machines (32 %), artificial neural networks (17 %) and k-nearest neighbours (12 %) dominate the field (Dladla and Thango, 2025a). The same trend appears more widely in power-system asset management, where artificial intelligence is increasingly used for condition monitoring and maintenance planning (Rajora *et al.*, 2024).

Despite this activity, most published ML studies for DGA stay as offline accuracy experiments. They are seldom compared fairly with the conventional methods on records whose fault labels are independent of those methods, and seldom placed in a working tool that an engineer could use next to the methods already trusted. This thesis addresses that gap with an integrated framework: conventional and ML diagnoses are produced from the same measured oil-sample record, compared with rigorous statistics, examined when fewer gases are available, and presented together in a simulated operating environment built in MATLAB/Simulink and Python.

## Problem Definition

Conventional DGA interpretation has two well-documented weaknesses. First, different methods frequently give different diagnoses for the same oil sample, because each was derived from a different population of faults and uses different limits (Suwarno *et al.*, 2024; Doğan *et al.*, 2026). Second, ratio methods may give no diagnosis at all when a ratio falls outside every coded range, and a sample close to a decision boundary can change class after a small change in one gas concentration (Zhang *et al.*, 2022; Suwarno *et al.*, 2024). @fig:duval-ch1 shows the Duval Triangle 1 with the inspection-confirmed IEC TC 10 cases: many points lie close to zone boundaries, where the method gives a categorical answer without any indication of how near the sample is to a different fault class.

![Duval Triangle 1 with the 117 inspection-confirmed cases of the IEC TC 10 database, coloured by the fault found at inspection](11_Results/figures/duval_benchmark.png){#fig:duval-ch1 width=12.5}

Machine learning has been proposed as a remedy, and many studies report accuracies above 90 % (Das *et al.*, 2023; Hechifa *et al.*, 2024; Adekunle *et al.*, 2025). These figures are hard to trust and hard to compare. The data sets are small, imbalanced and often of mixed origin; the origin of the fault labels is frequently undocumented, so labels may themselves come from a conventional method; and validation designs often allow information from the test data to leak into training (Rao *et al.*, 2021; Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023; Dladla and Thango, 2025a). In addition, online monitors installed on transformers often measure fewer gases than a laboratory analysis, which limits both the conventional methods and the models trained on full gas sets (Bustamante *et al.*, 2019). Finally, an ML classifier that exists only as an offline experiment gives an engineer no way to compare its answer with the conventional diagnosis, to see the transformer's operating condition, or to query it during routine work.

The problem addressed in this thesis is therefore the absence of a transparent and reproducible framework in which conventional DGA interpretation and ML diagnosis are produced from the same measured sample, compared statistically on data whose fault labels are independent of both, examined under reduced gas availability, and presented together to the engineer through a single interface.

## Objectives of the Research

The main objective of this research is to develop and rigorously evaluate a framework that diagnoses incipient power-transformer faults from measured dissolved-gas data using both conventional interpretation methods and machine learning, and to demonstrate it on an integrated MATLAB/Simulink–Python test bench. The specific objectives are to:

- implement the Rogers ratio, IEC 60599 ratio and Duval Triangle 1 methods in MATLAB and verify each implementation against published cases and an independent implementation;
- assemble a DGA data set from published sources in which the origin of every fault label is documented, and separate the records whose labels are independent of the conventional methods;
- train and compare five machine-learning classifiers using leakage-free nested cross-validation, and identify the gases that most influence the selected model;
- determine, by controlled removal of gases, the smallest gas panel with which the machine-learning model keeps an acceptable diagnostic performance;
- quantify and statistically test the agreement and disagreement between the machine-learning and conventional diagnoses, and test whether disagreement concentrates near the decision boundaries of the conventional methods; and
- integrate the conventional layer, the machine-learning model and a Simulink transformer operating model into one test bench with a diagnostic dashboard, and evaluate its fidelity and response time.

## Research Questions and Hypothesis

The research questions are:

- How does the accuracy of machine-learning classifiers compare with that of conventional DGA methods when both are tested on the same samples with independently confirmed faults?
- Where do the two approaches disagree, and is the disagreement concentrated near the decision boundaries of the conventional methods?
- How much accuracy is lost when the machine-learning model is given fewer gases, and what is the minimum useful gas panel?
- Can the conventional layer and the machine-learning model be served through one interface that returns exactly the same results as the offline analysis, with a response time suitable for interactive use?

The research hypothesis is: a machine-learning classifier validated without data leakage will diagnose more samples correctly than any single conventional method on the same data, mainly because it gives an answer where conventional methods give none, and the disagreement between the two will be concentrated near the conventional-method decision boundaries. The criteria that decide the hypothesis were fixed before any test result was computed (@sec:hyp-criteria).

## Scope of the Research

The research covers oil-immersed power transformers and the five hydrocarbon and hydrogen gases H2, CH4, C2H6, C2H4 and C2H2, which are the gases available in all the published data sets used. The fault classes are those of IEC 60599: partial discharge (PD), low-energy discharge (D1), high-energy discharge (D2) and thermal faults below 300 °C (T1), between 300 °C and 700 °C (T2) and above 700 °C (T3), together with a Normal class where the data provide one. The study is limited to published, real measured records; no gas concentration is simulated. The Simulink model represents the electrical and thermal operating context of one 40 MVA, 110/20 kV transformer and is used only for demonstration.

## Research Methods Used

The research methods used are:

- review of relevant literature and standards;
- data audit, cleaning and de-duplication of published DGA data sets with documented label provenance;
- implementation of the conventional methods in MATLAB, with an independent Python implementation as a cross-check;
- machine-learning model development with nested cross-validation and a written model-selection rule;
- statistical comparison (McNemar and Cochran tests, Cohen's kappa, logistic regression, bootstrap confidence intervals);
- MATLAB/Simulink and Simscape modelling of the transformer operating condition, verified against the IEC 60076-7 thermal equations; and
- software integration (FastAPI service and MATLAB App Designer dashboard) and end-to-end testing.

## Facilities Used for the Research

The facilities used are:

- a personal laptop computer (AMD Ryzen 5 3500U processor, 8 logical processors, about 22 GB of memory);
- MATLAB R2025b with Simulink, Simscape and Simscape Electrical; and
- a Python 3.12 environment with scikit-learn, XGBoost, SHAP and the FastAPI web framework, together with the library and internet facilities of UMaT.

## Significance of the Research

The research is significant in the following ways:

- it gives utility engineers, including those of Ghanaian utilities, a measured and honest picture of what machine learning adds to the conventional DGA methods they already use, based on inspection-confirmed cases rather than on accuracy figures from unclear data;
- it shows how much diagnostic ability remains when fewer gases are measured, which informs the choice of online monitors;
- it provides a reproducible template, with every number traceable to a saved file, for integrating data-driven diagnosis with conventional methods and a physical operating model; and
- it contributes evidence to the academic discussion on validation practice and data leakage in ML-based transformer diagnostics.

## Limitations of the Research

The study relies on published data rather than new utility records: the only data set with inspection-confirmed labels (IEC TC 10) is small, and the label origin of the other sets is not documented. The IEC 60599 ratio limits were refined using the same IEC TC 10 cases (Duval and dePablo, 2001), which gives the conventional methods a home advantage on the main benchmark. Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide could not be used because most records lack them. The full texts of IEC 60599:2022, IEEE C57.104-2019 and IEC 60076-7:2018 were not available; limits were taken from official previews, the 2008 IEEE guide and peer-reviewed secondary sources, and the thermal constants of the Simulink model are the default values of an open-source implementation of IEC 60076-7 rather than nameplate data. Response times were measured on a personal computer, not on a production server.

## Definition of Terms and Key Concepts

*Dissolved Gas Analysis:* A condition-monitoring technique that measures the gases dissolved in the insulating oil of a transformer to detect and identify incipient faults.

*Fault class:* A category of incipient fault defined in IEC 60599 (PD, D1, D2, T1, T2, T3), or Normal when no fault is present.

*Conventional method:* A rule-based DGA interpretation method that uses fixed gas-ratio limits or graphical zones, such as the Rogers ratio method, the IEC 60599 ratio method and the Duval Triangle 1.

*No diagnosis (ND):* The result of a conventional method when the sample matches none of its coded cases.

*Machine learning:* A family of methods in which a model learns the relationship between inputs and outputs from examples rather than from fixed rules.

*Nested cross-validation:* A validation design in which the settings of a model are tuned inside each training part of an outer cross-validation, so that the outer test folds are never used for tuning.

*Data leakage:* Any route by which information from the test data reaches the model during training or selection, making the measured performance optimistic.

*Macro-F1:* The unweighted mean of the per-class F1-scores; it gives every fault class the same importance.

*Fidelity:* The share of records for which the integrated system returns exactly the same diagnosis as the offline analysis.

*Simulink and Simscape:* MATLAB environments for block-diagram simulation; Simscape models physical networks (electrical, thermal) from component blocks.

## Organisation of the Thesis

The thesis has five chapters. Chapter 1 introduces the background, problem, objectives, research questions and hypothesis, scope, methods, significance and limitations. Chapter 2 reviews the literature on transformer faults and gas formation, the conventional DGA methods, machine learning for DGA, validation practice, online monitoring, thermal modelling and integrated diagnostic frameworks, and identifies the research gap.
