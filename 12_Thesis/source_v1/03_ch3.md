# Materials and Methods {#sec:ch3}

## Introduction

This chapter describes how the research was carried out. @fig:framework shows the framework. Published DGA records were audited, cleaned and frozen (Section 3.2). The three conventional methods were implemented in MATLAB (Section 3.3), and the machine-learning pipeline was built and validated in Python (Section 3.4). The comparison used the measures and tests of Section 3.5, and the reduced-gas study is described in Section 3.6. The Simulink operating model (Section 3.7), the diagnosis service (Section 3.8) and the MATLAB dashboard and test bench (Section 3.9) form the demonstration part of the framework. An exploratory improvement study is described in Section 3.10, and the safeguards for reproducibility in Section 3.11.

![The integrated framework: measured data, Python analysis and service, and the MATLAB/Simulink test bench](12_Thesis/figures/F3_1_framework.png){#fig:framework width=15.5}

Two rules governed the whole work. First, nothing was invented: every gas value is a published measurement, and the Simulink model never produces a gas value. Second, every analysis choice that could influence a result was written down before that result was computed, in an analysis plan approved before the work started and in a numbered decision log (D1 to D40). The decisions cited in this chapter refer to that log.

## Data

### Data Sources

Four published data sets were used (@tab:datasets). The IEC TC 10 database of Duval and dePablo (2001) contains the DGA results of faulty equipment inspected in service, with the fault found at inspection; it was transcribed from Annex 1 of the paper and cross-checked cell by cell. The two files of the Dissanayake (2026) data set and the Li (2019) data set were obtained from IEEE DataPort. All files were stored unchanged with their SHA-256 fingerprints (NIST, 2015).

[[TABLE:datasets]] {#tab:datasets}

The origin of the labels decided the role of each set. Only the IEC TC 10 labels are known to come from inspection and are therefore independent of the conventional methods; the IEC TC 10 database is the main benchmark. The labels of the Dissanayake and Li files are not documented. The Dissanayake training file was used for development (training, tuning and cross-validation) and its unseen file as an internal test set; the Li (2019) set was used as an external sensitivity test.

### Data Preparation

Each source was converted to one common table with the record identifier, the five gases in parts per million (ppm), the fault class and the data role. The following steps were applied, each with a log and an automated test:

- **Gases.** The study uses H2, CH4, C2H6, C2H4 and C2H2, the gases present in every source (decision D2). CO and CO2 were kept in the IEC TC 10 transcription for completeness only.
- **Fault classes.** Classes were coded 0 to 6 as Normal, PD, D1, D2, T1, T2 and T3. The IEC TC 10 tables print T1 and T2 together, so these cases form a merged class T1/T2. The Li labels were mapped as approved: partial discharge to PD, spark discharge to D1, arc discharge to D2, low-, middle- and high-temperature overheating to T1, T2 and T3, and low/middle-temperature overheating to T1/T2.
- **Values not available.** Dashes in the IEC TC 10 tables and the placeholder 0.0001 ppm in the Dissanayake files mean that the gas was not available. The missingness was informative: C2H2 was missing in most T1 records and never in D1 or D2, consistent with a gas that was not detected. Such values were therefore treated as not detected, 0 ppm, with flags kept for every record (decision D8); a complete-case analysis was kept as a sensitivity check.
- **Leakage between files.** The Dissanayake training file contained IEC TC 10 cases and records that match the test files. All training records that match a test or benchmark record were removed ({{n_removed_leak}} records), together with {{n_removed_copy}} exact copies and {{n_removed_conflict}} records with identical gases but conflicting labels.
- **Duplicates between test sets.** The Li (2019) file contains 115 of the 117 IEC TC 10 cases, and the Dissanayake unseen file contains 9 of them. Test records that copy a benchmark case were flagged; results reported as separate evidence use only the independent records (internal test {{n_int}} of {{n_int_all}}; Li {{n_li}} of {{n_li_all}}), and the full sets are reported as sensitivity analyses (decision D9).
- **Near-duplicates.** Records whose five gases agree within 1 % or 1 ppm were put in one group, and a group was never split between training and validation folds.

The result was a master table of {{n_master}} records. @fig:dataflow shows the flow of records from the raw files to the final roles, and @tab:records (Chapter 4) gives the records per class.

![Data preparation flow with the number of records at each stage](11_Results/figures/F3_2_data_preparation_flow.png){#fig:dataflow width=15}

### Evaluation Sets

Three primary evaluation sets were fixed before any test record was predicted (decision D19):

- the internal test set: {{n_int}} Dissanayake unseen records independent of the benchmark, seven classes;
- the IEC TC 10 benchmark: the {{n_iec}} power transformers (equipment codes P and U), scored on five merged classes PD, D1, D2, T1/T2 and T3; all {{n_iec_all}} cases form a sensitivity analysis; and
- the Li (2019) set: {{n_li}} records independent of the benchmark, six fault classes.

On the IEC TC 10 benchmark a prediction of T1 or T2 counts as T1/T2, and a Normal answer counts as wrong, because every benchmark case is a confirmed fault.

### Threats to Validity

Three threats were identified in advance and are reported with the results. First, the IEC 60599 ratio limits were refined using the IEC TC 10 cases (Duval and dePablo, 2001), so the conventional methods have a home advantage on the main benchmark. Second, the labels of the Dissanayake files are of unknown origin; the training file even contained IEC TC 10 cases whose combined T1/T2 label had been replaced by specific labels that the inspection does not support (decision D7). Third, some classes are small; for example, the {{n_iec}} benchmark power transformers include only one PD case, so per-class results have wide intervals.

### Data Freeze

After preparation, the data were frozen as data version 1.0: the SHA-256 fingerprints of all 16 data and standards files were stored, and every later program checks them before reading any data. The folds for cross-validation (5 folds × 10 repeats, stratified by class, with near-duplicate groups kept together, seed 20261010) were saved in the same version.

## Conventional Methods

The three conventional methods were implemented in MATLAB R2025b as functions that read their limits from the standards tables of the project; no limit is typed into the code. The tables were transcribed from the sources given under each table and checked against the worked examples of those sources.

### Gas Ratios

The ratio methods use three ratios:

$$R_1=(CH_4)/(H_2),  R_2=(C_2 H_2)/(C_2 H_4),  R_5=(C_2 H_4)/(C_2 H_6)$$ {#eq:ratios}

where:
: *R*~1~, *R*~2~, *R*~5~ = gas ratios (dimensionless), named as in IEEE Std C57.104
: H2, CH4, C2H2, C2H4, C2H6 = concentrations of the gases in the oil (ppm)

A ratio whose denominator is zero is undefined. A method returns no diagnosis (ND) when a ratio that the candidate class uses is undefined; ratios marked "not significant" for a class are not used (decision D12).

### IEC 60599 Ratio Method

@tab:iec gives the limits of IEC 60599. A class is assigned when all its significant ratios fall within the printed ranges. Two implementation rules were fixed (decisions D11 and D13). Ranges printed as "a – b" include both ends. Where the D1 and D2 rows overlap, the method returns the dual answer "D1/D2", as the standard allows; this counts as correct for a true D1 or D2. The T1 entry for CH4/H2, printed "> 1 but NS", is applied as CH4/H2 > 1. Read literally as "not significant", every PD sample would also match T1, and the method could never answer PD alone.

[[TABLE:iec_table]] {#tab:iec}

### Rogers Ratio Method

The Rogers method follows Table 6 of IEEE Std C57.104-2008 (@tab:rogers). Its cases were mapped to the project classes: case 0 to Normal, 1 to PD, 2 to D2, 3 to T1, 4 to T2 and 5 to T3. The method has no D1 case, which is a limitation when it is scored against D1 records.

[[TABLE:rogers_table]] {#tab:rogers}

### Duval Triangle 1

The Duval Triangle 1 uses the shares of CH4, C2H4 and C2H2 in their sum:

$$%CH_4=100 (CH_4)/(CH_4+C_2 H_4+C_2 H_2)$$ {#eq:duvalpct}

where:
: %CH4 = share of methane in the sum of the three gases (%); %C2H4 and %C2H2 are defined in the same way, and the three shares add up to 100 %

For plotting, a sample is placed in an equilateral triangle of side 1 by

$$x=(%C_2 H_4+0.5 %CH_4)/100,  y=(√3/2)(%CH_4/100)$$ {#eq:duvalxy}

where:
: *x*, *y* = coordinates of the sample point; *x* = 0 at the 100 % C2H2 corner and 1 at the 100 % C2H4 corner, *y* = √3/2 at the 100 % CH4 corner

The zone rules (@tab:duval) are tested in order, and the first rule that holds gives the zone. They were taken from a secondary source that cites the zone limits of IEEE Std C57.104-2019 (Moser, 2025), and the zone labels were checked against an open-access figure (Manisha *et al.*, 2022). Together the rules cover the triangle without gaps or overlaps and reproduce 39 of 39 printed example diagnoses. The DT zone has no matching fault class in the data; a DT answer counts as a diagnosis but as a wrong one, and DT answers are reported separately.

[[TABLE:duval_table]] {#tab:duval}

### Normality Gate

The conventional methods cannot by themselves answer "Normal". IEC 60599 gives ranges of 90 % typical concentrations (@tab:typical), and gas ratios of equipment with gases below these values have no diagnostic meaning (Duval and dePablo, 2001). A common normality gate was therefore applied to all three methods (decision D11):

$$y="Normal"  "if"  x_g≤x_g^"typ"  "for every gas " g$$ {#eq:gate}

where:
: *y* = answer of the conventional method
: *x*~g~ = measured concentration of gas *g* (ppm)
: *x*~g~^typ^ = upper end of the 90 % typical range of gas *g* for the equipment type (ppm)

The upper end of each range is the primary rule; the lower end and no gate at all were run as sensitivity analyses.

[[TABLE:typical_table]] {#tab:typical}

### No Diagnosis, Coverage and Boundary Distance

The share of records without a diagnosis is

$$U=n_"ND"/N,  "Coverage"=1-U$$ {#eq:coverage}

where:
: *U* = unresolved (no-diagnosis) rate
: *n*~ND~ = number of records for which the method gives no diagnosis
: *N* = number of records

To test whether disagreement concentrates near the decision boundaries, the distance of each record to the nearest boundary of each method was computed. For the ratio methods the distance is measured in decades of the ratio:

$$d_R=min┬(j,l)⁡|log_10⁡ R_j-log_10⁡ L_(j,l)|$$ {#eq:dist-ratio}

where:
: *d*~R~ = distance to the nearest ratio limit (decades)
: *R*~j~ = value of ratio *j* of the record; *L*~j,l~ = the *l*-th limit of ratio *j* in the code table

For the Duval Triangle the distance is the shortest Euclidean distance, in percentage points, from the sample point *P* to an edge that separates two different zones (decision D12):

$$d_D=min┬(e∈E) "dist"(P,e)$$ {#eq:dist-duval}

where:
: *d*~D~ = distance to the nearest zone boundary (percentage points)
: *E* = set of zone edges that separate two different zones; dist(*P*, *e*) = shortest distance from *P* to the segment *e*

### Verification of the Implementation

The MATLAB functions were checked by 67 unit tests and against the worked examples of the sources. The three methods were then implemented a second time, independently, in Python from the same tables and rules. The two implementations agreed on all 2 481 answers for the {{n_master}} records (three methods each), and their numerical outputs agreed within 10^-9^. The final answers of the methods over all records are summarised in @tab:conventional (Chapter 4).

## Machine-Learning Pipeline

The pipeline was written in Python with scikit-learn 1.9.1 (Pedregosa *et al.*, 2011). All settings are in one configuration file, and every program first checks the data version.

### Features

Gas concentrations are strongly skewed, so four feature sets were built (@tab:features):

$$u_g=log_10⁡(x_g+1)$$ {#eq:loggas}

$$p_g=100 x_g/(∑_(h=1)^5 x_h)$$ {#eq:pctgas}

$$r_k=log_10⁡((a_k+1)/(b_k+1))$$ {#eq:logratio}

where:
: *u*~g~ = log-transformed concentration of gas *g*; the offset of 1 ppm keeps a zero finite
: *p*~g~ = share of gas *g* in the total of the five gases (%)
: *r*~k~ = log-ratio *k* (*k* = 1, …, 5) of gases *a*~k~ and *b*~k~: R1 = CH4/H2, R2 = C2H2/C2H4, R3 = C2H2/CH4, R4 = C2H6/C2H2, R5 = C2H4/C2H6 (decision D15)

[[TABLE:feature_sets]] {#tab:features}

Each feature was standardised with the mean and standard deviation of the training fold, *z* = (*x* − *μ*)/*σ*. Undefined Duval percentages (two development records with CH4 = C2H4 = C2H2 = 0) were replaced by the median of the training fold.

### Classifiers

Five classifiers were compared, with a majority-class baseline as a floor (decision D6; @tab:grids): a support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel and Platt-scaled probabilities (Platt, 1999), a random forest of 500 trees (Breiman, 2001), XGBoost gradient-boosted trees (Chen and Guestrin, 2016), a multilayer perceptron trained with L-BFGS (Liu and Nocedal, 1989) and multinomial logistic regression as an interpretable linear model.

[[TABLE:grids]] {#tab:grids}

Class imbalance was handled with class weights recomputed in every training fold, so that each class carries the same total weight:

$$w_c=N/(K n_c)$$ {#eq:weights}

where:
: *w*~c~ = weight of every training record of class *c*
: *N* = number of training records; *K* = number of classes; *n*~c~ = number of training records of class *c*

Over-sampling (SMOTE) was not used.

### Nested Cross-Validation and Model Selection

Each of the 24 model–feature combinations was evaluated by nested cross-validation (Varma and Simon, 2006) on the {{n_dev}} development records (@fig:nestedcv). The outer loop used the saved folds (5 folds × 10 repeats = 50 test folds). Inside each outer training part, an inner StratifiedGroupKFold(5) grid search chose the settings by macro-F1, and the chosen setting was refitted on the whole outer training part and scored on the outer test fold (decision D17). The test sets were not read.

![Nested cross-validation scheme](12_Thesis/figures/F3_3_nested_cv.png){#fig:nestedcv width=15}

The selection rule was written before any cross-validation result was read (decision D18). The pipeline with the highest mean macro-F1 is compared with the next best using the corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau and Bengio, 2003):

$$t=d̄/√((1/(k r)+n_"test"/n_"train") σ_d^2)$$ {#eq:cr-ttest}

where:
: *d̄* = mean over the 50 folds of the difference in macro-F1 between the two pipelines; *σ*~d~^2^ = variance of the differences
: *k* = 5 folds, *r* = 10 repeats; *n*~test~/*n*~train~ = ratio of test to training size ({{sel_ratio}}); *t* is compared with a *t* distribution with 49 degrees of freedom

If the difference is not significant at 0.05, the simpler pipeline is chosen: by model family (logistic regression < SVM < random forest < XGBoost < MLP), then by the number of features.

### Final Model and One-Time Testing

The selected pipeline was tuned once more on all development records with the same grid and inner procedure, refitted, fingerprinted and saved before any test record was predicted (decision D19). The {{n_heldout}} test records were then predicted in one run. The program logs every access to the test sets and refuses to run a second time, so no choice could be changed after the test results were seen. The fitted pipeline was released unchanged as dga_model_v1, with a model card (Mitchell *et al.*, 2019), and is loaded only through a function that checks its SHA-256 fingerprint.

### Explainability

Two methods explain the selected model (decision D20). Permutation importance measures the drop of macro-F1 when the values of one input are shuffled:

$$I_j=s-(1/R)∑_(r=1)^R s_(r,j)$$ {#eq:perm}

where:
: *I*~j~ = importance of input *j*; *s* = macro-F1 on the original data; *s*~r,j~ = macro-F1 after shuffling input *j* in repeat *r*; *R* = 30 repeats

Because the 18 features are derived from five gases and are strongly correlated, single-feature shuffling understates shared information (Strobl *et al.*, 2008). The main ranking therefore shuffles whole gases at the input of the pipeline, so that every feature derived from a gas moves with it. Exact TreeSHAP values (Lundberg *et al.*, 2020) gave each feature's contribution to the predicted probability of each class, and the mean absolute SHAP value ranked the features. Before the values were computed, the expected key gases of each fault type were written down: H2 for PD, C2H2 for D1 and D2, CH4 or C2H6 for T1, C2H4 or CH4 for T2, and C2H4 for T3. For each fault class it was then checked whether one of the three most important features involves an expected gas.

## Performance Measures and Statistical Tests

### Measures

All measures derive from the confusion matrix of true against predicted classes:

$$"Prec"_c=TP_c/(TP_c+FP_c),  "Rec"_c=TP_c/(TP_c+FN_c),  "F1"_c=(2 "Prec"_c "Rec"_c)/("Prec"_c+"Rec"_c)$$ {#eq:prf}

$$"Macro-F1"=1/K ∑_(c=1)^K "F1"_c,  "Balanced accuracy"=1/K ∑_(c=1)^K "Rec"_c$$ {#eq:macro}

where:
: *TP*~c~, *FP*~c~, *FN*~c~ = true positives, false positives and false negatives of class *c* (a no-diagnosis counts as a false negative)
: *K* = number of classes in the evaluation set

Macro-F1 is the primary measure because it gives every fault class equal weight. For the conventional methods, strict and conditional accuracy are reported together with coverage:

$$A_"strict"=n_"correct"/N,  A_"cond"=n_"correct"/(N-n_"ND")$$ {#eq:accuracy}

where:
: *A*~strict~ = strict accuracy, every no-diagnosis counted as wrong; *A*~cond~ = conditional accuracy over the records that received a diagnosis; *n*~correct~ = number of correct diagnoses

Agreement between two raters (two methods, or a method and the truth) was measured with Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960), and overall classification quality also with the multi-class Matthews correlation coefficient (Gorodkin, 2004):

$$κ=(p_o-p_e)/(1-p_e)$$ {#eq:kappa}

where:
: *p*~o~ = observed proportion of agreement; *p*~e~ = proportion of agreement expected by chance

### Confidence Intervals

Proportions such as accuracy and coverage carry Wilson score intervals (Wilson, 1927):

$$"CI"_95=(p+z^2/(2n)±z√(p(1-p)/n+z^2/(4n^2)))/(1+z^2/n)$$ {#eq:wilson}

where:
: *p* = observed proportion; *n* = number of records; *z* = 1.96

Macro-F1 and the differences between methods carry stratified percentile bootstrap intervals (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993) with *B* = 2 000 resamples drawn with replacement within each true class; the same resamples are used for all methods, so that differences are paired.

### Comparing the Classifiers

The six models on feature set F4 were ranked within each outer fold, and the Friedman test (Friedman, 1937) checked whether their average ranks differ. Pairs were then compared with the Nemenyi critical difference (Demšar, 2006):

$$"CD"=q_α √(k(k+1)/(6 n_f))$$ {#eq:cd}

where:
: *CD* = critical difference of average ranks; *q*~α~ = studentised range value for *k* models at *α* = 0.05, divided by √2; *n*~f~ = 50 folds

### Comparing the ML Model with the Conventional Methods

On each primary test set every method is either right or wrong on each record (no-diagnosis counts as wrong). Cochran's Q test (Cochran, 1950) first checks whether the four methods have the same proportion correct:

$$Q=(m-1)(m∑_(j=1)^m C_j^2-T^2)/(m T-∑_(i=1)^N a_i^2)$$ {#eq:cochran}

where:
: *m* = 4 methods; *C*~j~ = number of records method *j* diagnosed correctly; *a*~i~ = number of methods correct on record *i*; *T* = ∑*C*~j~

The ML model was then compared with each conventional method by McNemar's test (McNemar, 1947), which uses only the discordant records:

$$χ^2=(|b-c|-1)^2/(b+c)$$ {#eq:mcnemar}

where:
: *b* = records the ML model diagnosed correctly and the conventional method did not; *c* = the reverse

When *b* + *c* < 25 the exact binomial test was used instead. The three p-values on each test set were adjusted by Holm's procedure (Holm, 1979), which rejects the *i*-th smallest p-value while *p*~i~ ≤ *α*/(*m* − *i* + 1) with *m* = 3. The effect size is the odds ratio *b*/*c* with its 95 % interval.

### The Hypothesis and the Boundary Analysis {#sec:hyp-criteria}

The hypothesis is supported only if all three criteria hold on the main benchmark (IEC TC 10 power transformers); otherwise it is reported as partly supported or not supported:

1. the ML model has a higher strict accuracy than each conventional method, with a Holm-adjusted McNemar p-value below 0.05;
2. the advantage comes mainly from the records on which a method gives no diagnosis, that is, the gap in conditional accuracy is smaller than the gap in strict accuracy; and
3. disagreement between ML and each method becomes more likely closer to the method's decision boundary.

Criterion 3 was tested by logistic regression of disagreement on the standardised boundary distance (decision D27):

$$ln(π_i/(1-π_i))=β_0+β_1 d_i,  "OR"=e^(β_1)$$ {#eq:logit}

where:
: *π*~i~ = probability that the ML answer and the method's answer differ for record *i*
: *d*~i~ = boundary distance of record *i* (@eq:dist-ratio or @eq:dist-duval), standardised to a z-score
: *β*~1~ = change of the log-odds per standard deviation of distance; *OR* = odds ratio

Criterion 3 holds for a method when *β*~1~ < 0 and its 95 % interval excludes zero. The 205 records of the three primary test sets were pooled for the main estimate; the answers after the normality gate were primary, and the raw answers (no gate) a sensitivity analysis, because a Normal answer produced by the gate is not caused by a ratio or zone boundary.

## Reduced-Gas Study

With five gases there are 2^5^ − 1 = 31 non-empty subsets. For every subset *S* the selected pipeline (random forest on F4, same grid) was re-run through the same nested cross-validation, using only what *S* allows: the log of each gas in *S*, the shares within the gases of *S*, a ratio only when both of its gases are in *S*, and the Duval percentages only when CH4, C2H4 and C2H2 are all present (decision D22). With all five gases this is exactly F4. The loss is

$$Δ_S="Macro-F1"_"full"-"Macro-F1"_S$$ {#eq:loss}

and the minimum panel is the subset with the fewest gases whose upper 95 % bootstrap limit of the loss is at most the tolerance δ = 0.05, fixed in advance (decision D24):

$$U_95(Δ_S)≤δ$$ {#eq:panel}

where:
: *Δ*~S~ = loss of macro-F1 with only the gases in *S*; *U*~95~ = upper limit of the 95 % paired bootstrap interval (*B* = 2 000)

For the conventional methods applicability follows from their formulas: the ratio methods need all five gases and the Duval Triangle needs CH4, C2H4 and C2H2. This was confirmed by running the MATLAB implementations with removed gases set to "not measured" (decision D23).

## Simulink Operating Model

### Purpose and Structure

The Simulink model represents the operating context of one power transformer: its load, its oil and hot-spot temperatures and the ageing of its insulation. It never generates a gas value (decision D4); the gases always come from the measured records. The model is built by a MATLAB script from a build guide, so that every block, parameter and connection is reviewable, and all parameters are variables in one file (@tab:simparams).

[[TABLE:sim_params]] {#tab:simparams}

@fig:simtop shows the top level of the model in MATLAB R2025b. It has eight subsystems. SS1 to SS4 form a Simscape Electrical network: a 110 kV grid supply (SS1), the 40 MVA 110/20 kV two-winding transformer (SS2), measurement of the low-voltage current and the load factor *K* (SS3) and a variable load (SS4). SS5 converts the losses into heat inputs, SS6 is a Simscape thermal network that solves the IEC 60076-7 equations as resistance–capacitance circuits, and SS7 computes the outputs and the ageing. SS8 produces the DGA sampling events. Because the electrical network uses Simscape's frequency-and-time formulation, which accepts only linear equations, the non-linear arithmetic of SS5 and SS7 is done in Simulink blocks; the model is therefore "Simscape networks with Simulink arithmetic" (decision D31). Diagrams of all subsystems are given in @app:simulink.

![Top level of the Simulink operating model (Transformer_Operating_Model.slx, MATLAB R2025b). Blue: Simscape Electrical network (SS1–SS4); green: Simulink arithmetic (SS5, SS7); orange: Simscape thermal network (SS6); purple: DGA sampling events (SS8). Blue lines are physical connections, black lines Simulink signals](12_Thesis/figures/simulink_top_level_blocks.png){#fig:simtop width=15.5}

![Simscape thermal network of subsystem SS6, which solves the top-oil and hot-spot equations as thermal resistance–capacitance circuits](12_Thesis/figures/simulink_SS6_Thermal_Network_crop.png){#fig:ss6 width=11 maxh=13}

### Thermal Equations

The thermal network implements the differential equations of IEC 60076-7 (IEC, 2018). The top-oil temperature follows

$$k_11 τ_o (dθ_o)/dt=((1+K^2 R)/(1+R))^x Δθ_or-(θ_o-θ_a)$$ {#eq:topoil}

and the hot-spot temperature is the top-oil temperature plus the difference of two first-order components:

$$k_21 Δθ_hr K^y=k_22 τ_w (dΔθ_h1)/dt+Δθ_h1,  (k_21-1) Δθ_hr K^y=(τ_o/k_22) (dΔθ_h2)/dt+Δθ_h2$$ {#eq:hotspot}

$$θ_h=θ_o+Δθ_h1-Δθ_h2,  Δθ_hr=H g_r,  R=P_k/P_0$$ {#eq:theta-h}

where:
: *θ*~o~, *θ*~h~, *θ*~a~ = top-oil, hot-spot and ambient temperature (°C); *t* = time (min)
: *K* = load factor (pu); *R* = ratio of load loss to no-load loss (7.556)
: *k*~11~, *k*~21~, *k*~22~, *τ*~o~, *τ*~w~, *x*, *y*, *Δθ*~or~, *g*~r~, *H* = constants of @tab:simparams
: *Δθ*~h1~, *Δθ*~h2~ = the two components of the hot-spot-to-top-oil gradient (K)

The relative ageing rate of the paper and the loss of life are

$$V=2^((θ_h-98)/6),  L=∫_(t_1)^(t_2) V dt$$ {#eq:ageing}

where:
: *V* = relative ageing rate (1 at a hot-spot temperature of 98 °C); *L* = loss of life over the period (h)

This is the ageing form for non-thermally-upgraded paper; its constants were taken from the implementation recorded in the parameter file. The thermal constants are the ONAF default values of an open-source implementation of IEC 60076-7 (Alliander transformer-thermal-model), and the electrical data are those of a standard 40 MVA, 110/20 kV type in the pandapower library; they describe a typical unit, not a specific nameplate.

### Verification and Scenarios

The model was verified against an independent numerical solution of the thermal equations (@eq:topoil to @eq:theta-h), solved with a stiff ODE solver at tight tolerance and driven by the load factor measured in the model (decision D33). Two tests were run: rated load from a cold start (ambient 20 °C, 24 h) and a load step from 0.6 to 1.2 pu at 6 h. The measures were

$$e_"max"=max┬t |θ_"sim"(t)-θ_"ref"(t)|,  "RMSE"=√(1/M ∑_(m=1)^M (θ_"sim"(t_m)-θ_"ref"(t_m))^2)$$ {#eq:emax}

where:
: *θ*~sim~, *θ*~ref~ = top-oil or hot-spot temperature from the model and from the reference (°C); *M* = number of time points after the first 10 min

The acceptance criterion, approved before the run, was *e*~max~ ≤ 0.1 K for both temperatures in both tests, and a 63 % top-oil rise time within 1 min of *k*~11~*τ*~o~ = 75 min. The model was also checked with the Simulink Model Advisor (132 modelling checks). Three illustrative operating scenarios were then simulated: constant rated load for 24 h, a daily load cycle (0.5 pu at night to 1.0 pu at the evening peak, repeated for three days, last day shown) and a short overload of 1.3 pu for 2 h on a base of 0.8 pu (decision D34). The scenario values are illustrative choices, not measured load data.

### DGA Sampling Events

Subsystem SS8 marks the times at which an oil sample is taken (6, 12, 18 and 22 h in the demonstration). A Simulink signal is numeric, so SS8 outputs the number of sampling events passed; outside the model this index selects the identifier of the next held-out record, and the dashboard reads that record's measured gases from the data file (decision D35). The parameter file reads only the record identifiers and data roles, so no gas value enters the model's workspace. A test confirmed that no block, parameter or logged signal of the model refers to a gas concentration.

## Diagnosis Service

The released model is served by a web service written with FastAPI (decision D36; @tab:api). The request carries the five gases in ppm and, optionally, a record identifier that is returned unchanged. Inputs are validated strictly: every gas is required and must be a finite number not below zero, and unknown fields are refused, so that a misspelt gas name cannot be ignored silently. Invalid requests receive HTTP status 422 with the field and the reason (Fielding, Nottingham and Reschke, 2022). The response gives the predicted class, the probabilities of all seven classes unrounded (so that they can be compared exactly with the offline results), the confidence, and the model name, version and fingerprint. The service loads the model once at start-up through the fingerprint check and refuses to start if the file has changed. It listens only on the local address 127.0.0.1, port 8000; it is a local test bench, not a networked product. Its interface description is written automatically in the OpenAPI 3.1 format, and 21 automated tests cover validation, answers and start-up.

## Dashboard and Test Bench

### Dashboard

The dashboard was built with MATLAB App Designer (decision D37). It loads a DGA record, computes the three conventional diagnoses with the MATLAB functions of Section 3.3, sends the five gases to the service and shows the answers side by side with the class probabilities, the sample in the Duval Triangle and an agreement lamp: green when all three conventional answers agree with the ML class, amber when at least one does not, and grey when the service is not reachable. A conventional answer agrees with ML when it equals the ML class; the IEC dual answer D1/D2 agrees when ML says D1 or D2; DT and ND never agree. The Run Simulation button runs the Simulink model for a simulated day, plots the top-oil and hot-spot temperatures as operating context, and at each sampling event looks up the record, diagnoses it and lists it in an event table (decision D38). @fig:dashboard-raw shows the dashboard after a simulated day; its parts are described with the annotated version in Chapter 4.

### End-to-End Test Bench and Integration Measures

For the formal evaluation, a test bench ran the integrated system over all {{n_heldout}} held-out records (decision D39). The unchanged Simulink model was simulated with one sampling event every 15 min, and for each event the bench called exactly the functions that the dashboard uses, without drawing, and logged the answers and the response time. The response time is the time between sending the request to the service and receiving the answer, measured in MATLAB around the web call. The fidelity of the system is

$$F=n_"match"/N$$ {#eq:fidelity}

where:
: *F* = fidelity; *n*~match~ = held-out records for which the logged ML class equals the offline prediction; *N* = held-out records sent

The approved requirements were *F* = 1 and a 95th-percentile response time below 1 000 ms (decision D40). The logged probabilities and conventional answers were also compared with the offline results.

## Exploratory Improvement Study

After the confirmatory results were known, an exploratory study (Study 2) was carried out to see how the model could be improved. Its protocol was registered before any Study 2 model was trained (decision D28), and the confirmatory results were not changed. The design was leave-one-source-out over four de-duplicated sources (Dissanayake training, Dissanayake unseen, IEC TC 10 power transformers and Li 2019), with T1 and T2 merged. Three models were compared: A, the released model unchanged; B, the same pipeline retrained on the other sources; and C, a hybrid that adds the conventional answers, gate flags and boundary distances to the inputs of B. A reject option was also examined: the hybrid model abstains on its least confident records until its coverage equals that of a ratio method (Chow, 1970), and its accuracy on the records it answers is compared with the method's conditional accuracy. Because the IEC TC 10 and Li sets had already been seen, Study 2 is labelled exploratory; a positive finding would need confirmation on fresh inspection-confirmed data.
