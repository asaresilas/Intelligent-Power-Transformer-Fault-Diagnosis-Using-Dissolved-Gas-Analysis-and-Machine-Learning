# Literature Review {#sec:ch2}

## Introduction

This chapter reviews the knowledge on which the study builds and the gaps it addresses. It starts with the failure of power transformers and the formation of fault gases in their insulation (Sections 2.2 and 2.3). It then reviews the conventional methods for interpreting dissolved gases and their known weaknesses (Section 2.4), the use of machine learning for DGA (Section 2.5), and the validation practices that determine whether reported results can be trusted (Section 2.6). Online monitoring with reduced gas sets, thermal modelling of transformers and integrated diagnostic frameworks are covered in Sections 2.7 to 2.9. The chapter ends with the research gap that this thesis addresses (Section 2.10).

## Power Transformers and Their Failures

A power transformer consists of copper or aluminium windings on a laminated steel core, insulated by cellulose paper and pressboard and immersed in mineral oil that both insulates and cools. With proper maintenance such units operate for 25 to 50 years, but internal stresses (insulation degradation, partial discharges, local overheating) and external events (lightning, switching surges, overloads) accelerate ageing and increase the risk of failure (Kashiwagi *et al.*, 2025).

Because major failures are rare in any single utility, reliable statistics need surveys that pool many utilities. The CIGRE working group A2.37 collected 964 major failures that occurred between 1996 and 2010 in a population of 167 459 transformer-years from 58 utilities in 21 countries; the failure rates of substation transformers were all below 1 % per year, winding, tap-changer and bushing failures were the main contributors, and dielectric failure modes were the most frequent (Tenbohlen, Jagers and Vahidi, 2017). A later analysis of CIGRE surveys covering 50 utilities, 26 533 transformers and 331 379 operating years reported 1 358 major failures and an overall major failure rate of 0.41 % per year (Martin and Tenbohlen, 2026). National studies give similar pictures: a survey of Australian utilities updated the statistics of failures and retirements of the national fleet (Martin, Marks and Saha, 2017), and an Indian utility study of 343 failures of transformers between 33 kV and 400 kV grouped the causes into electrical, thermal, mechanical and insulation-degradation failures and proposed a health-index approach to maintenance (Murugan and Ramasamy, 2019).

The cost of failure goes beyond the transformer itself. Failures of mineral-oil-filled transformers have been involved in numerous fire and explosion incidents, and insurance loss statistics for 1990 to 2000 recorded close to 1 000 transformer failures at customer facilities with a gross loss of US$492 million (Su, Madan and Purushothaman, 2024). These statistics explain the effort put into condition monitoring, and in particular into DGA, which can detect a developing fault while the transformer is still in service.

## Insulation Degradation and Fault Gases

### Formation of Fault Gases

Under thermal and electrical stress both the oil and the paper insulation decompose and release gases that dissolve in the oil (Suwarno *et al.*, 2024). The gases of interest are hydrogen (H2), methane (CH4), ethane (C2H6), ethylene (C2H4), acetylene (C2H2), carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO2). Partial discharge produces mainly H2 with a smaller amount of CH4; overheating of the oil produces CH4, C2H6 and C2H4; arcing produces C2H2; and CO and CO2 indicate degradation of the paper (Suwarno *et al.*, 2024).

The link between the temperature of a fault and the mixture of gases has a thermodynamic basis. Thermodynamic calculations of the thermal decomposition of alkane molecules, with temperature-dependent equilibrium constants, reproduce the gas generation observed in transformer oil and link the mixture of gases to the fault temperature (Wang *et al.*, 2024). Gases can also form without a fault: mineral oils can produce gases at relatively low temperatures (100 °C to 200 °C), and this "stray gassing" can be difficult to distinguish from a real thermal fault (Martin *et al.*, 2014). Duval (2002) reviewed the faults that can be detected by gas-in-oil analysis, and this review underlies the fault classes used today.

### Fault Classes

IEC 60599 groups the faults detectable by DGA into six basic types (IEC, 2007): partial discharges (PD); discharges of low energy (D1); discharges of high energy (D2); thermal faults below 300 °C (T1); thermal faults between 300 °C and 700 °C (T2); and thermal faults above 700 °C (T3). The same six types are used in most of the literature reviewed below (Ward *et al.*, 2021). @tab:gases summarises the main gases associated with each fault type.

## Conventional DGA Interpretation Methods

### Overview

Several interpretation methods are in industrial use: the key gas method, the Doernenburg ratio method, the Rogers ratio method, the IEC 60599 ratio method, the Duval Triangle and the Duval Pentagons (Ali *et al.*, 2023; Rangel Bessa *et al.*, 2023). Ratio methods compare pairs of gases, so that the result does not depend on the total amount of gas; graphical methods place a sample in a triangle or pentagon divided into fault zones. Before any of these methods is applied, IEC 60599 recommends checking whether the gas levels are above typical values for the equipment, because gas ratios of normal equipment have no diagnostic meaning (Duval and dePablo, 2001).

### Rogers Ratio Method

The Rogers method maps combinations of gas-ratio ranges to coded diagnoses (Rogers, 1978). The version given in IEEE Std C57.104-2008 uses three ratios, CH4/H2, C2H2/C2H4 and C2H4/C2H6, and has six cases: normal, low-energy partial discharge, high-energy arcing, and three thermal cases (IEEE, 2009). A sample whose ratios fall in a combination that is not coded receives no diagnosis. Two secondary reproductions of the IEEE table were checked in this study and both contained an error (Section 3.3), which shows how easily such tables are mis-transcribed.

### IEC 60599 Ratio Method

The IEC 60599 method uses the same three ratios as Rogers, but its limits were made more precise using the cases of the IEC TC 10 database (Duval and dePablo, 2001). Its Table 2 defines the six basic faults; ratios marked "not significant" are ignored for a given fault, and the D1 and D2 zones overlap, so that in some cases a dual attribution "D1 or D2" must be given (IEC, 2007). A simplified scheme is offered for cases that match no row of the main table.

### Duval Triangle 1

The Duval Triangle 1 uses only CH4, C2H4 and C2H2. Their relative percentages place a sample at one point of an equilateral triangle divided into seven zones: PD, D1, D2, T1, T2, T3 and DT, a mixture of thermal and electrical faults (Duval, 2002). Because every sample falls in some zone, the triangle always gives an answer, and it is regarded as one of the most reliable interpretation methods (Ekojono *et al.*, 2022). It has two limitations. The triangle has no normal zone, so it cannot by itself say that a transformer is healthy (Suwarno *et al.*, 2024). It also ignores H2, which matters for low-temperature thermal faults and partial discharges (Selim *et al.*, 2025). The Duval Pentagon method was later added to the graphical methods (Ali *et al.*, 2023), and some newer methods combine several Duval triangles (Manoj *et al.*, 2023).

### The IEC TC 10 Database

Duval and dePablo (2001) published the IEC TC 10 database: DGA results of faulty equipment that was inspected in service, with the fault identified by visual inspection. Because the labels come from inspection and not from a ratio method, the database is the standard benchmark for interpretation methods, and many studies use it (Rao *et al.*, 2021; Nanfak *et al.*, 2021; Das *et al.*, 2023; Rangel Bessa *et al.*, 2023). However, the same database was used to refine the IEC 60599 limits (Duval and dePablo, 2001). Conventional methods therefore have a "home advantage" on it, a point that is seldom stated when ML results on IEC TC 10 are compared with them.

### Limitations of the Conventional Methods

The literature agrees on three limitations. First, the methods may give different conclusions for the same oil sample (Suwarno *et al.*, 2024; Doğan *et al.*, 2026). Second, ratio-based methods may return an out-of-code result when a ratio falls outside the specified ranges, and a large share of fault cases may be left unidentified (Suwarno *et al.*, 2024; Selim *et al.*, 2025). Third, the methods rely on fixed thresholds and give categorical answers; they offer little support for expressing uncertainty, which modern prognostics and health management requires (Kashiwagi *et al.*, 2025). The fixed boundaries also mean that a sample near a boundary can change class after a small change in a measured gas, which is the "boundary problem" mentioned in reviews of intelligent methods (Zhang *et al.*, 2022).

Reported accuracies of the conventional methods vary widely between data sets. In a comparison of seven methods on data from two Pakistani grid stations, the three-ratio method was the most accurate, with 90 % (Wajid *et al.*, 2023). On a pooled data set of 282 samples, the Duval Triangle and Pentagon achieved only 35.8 % and 30.8 % (Dladla and Thango, 2025b). New methods continue to be proposed, such as a method based on subsets of gases tested on IEC TC 10 (Nanfak *et al.*, 2021), a graphical shape in the Cartesian plane that adds H2 and C2H6 (Selim *et al.*, 2025), and fault-specific gas limits (Duval and Paulhiac, 2026). This variety shows that no conventional method is accepted as clearly best.

## Machine Learning for DGA Fault Diagnosis

### Classifiers Used

A systematic review of 124 papers published between 2014 and 2024 found that support vector machines (32 %), artificial neural networks (17 %) and k-nearest neighbours (12 %) are the most used algorithms (Dladla and Thango, 2025a). Earlier surveys covered expert systems, fuzzy logic, rough sets, grey systems and swarm-optimised classifiers and concluded that no single intelligent approach resolves all the shortcomings of DGA interpretation (Cheng and Yu, 2018; Zhang *et al.*, 2022). More recent work has moved towards ensembles of decision trees. Using 4 580 DGA samples from five utilities for training and the IEC TC 10 database for testing, Rao *et al.* (2021) found that non-parametric methods and non-linear SVM suit DGA classification and that ensemble methods performed best. Random forests performed best among six algorithms trained on Duval-triangle fault codes (Ekojono *et al.*, 2022). Random forest, XGBoost and LightGBM consistently outperformed non-ensemble models across four ratio-based feature schemes (Adekunle *et al.*, 2025), and LightGBM and random forest led a benchmark of twelve models under one evaluation protocol (Doğan *et al.*, 2026).

Deep learning has also been applied. A convolutional neural network with hybrid ratio inputs, tested with noise added to the measurements, gave higher accuracy than conventional ratio inputs (Taha, Ibrahim and Mansour, 2021), and a convolutional network for online DGA data classified faults with about 87 % accuracy (Jin *et al.*, 2024). However, a review that re-tested shallow and deep learning on four DGA data sets found that deep learning did not significantly outperform shallow learning and could perform worse, while needing far more parameters (Chen and Zhang, 2025). Logistic regression, a simple linear baseline from statistics, is rarely reported in DGA studies, although such baselines are needed to judge whether complex models add value.

Two observations follow from @tab:ml-studies. First, studies that test on an independent database or that use a strict protocol tend to report lower figures than studies that split one pooled data set: compare the validation accuracy of Hechifa *et al.* (2024) with their test accuracy, or the 68.1 % of Nkwambe and Thango (2026) with the near-perfect results elsewhere. Second, few studies report measures that treat all fault classes equally, such as macro-F1 or balanced accuracy, although DGA data sets are strongly imbalanced (Dladla and Thango, 2025a; Doğan *et al.*, 2026).

### Features and Hybrid Methods

Engineered inputs strongly affect performance. Gas ratios and percentages from the Duval, Doernenburg and Rogers methods are common inputs (Al-Sakini *et al.*, 2024), and among 22 feature vectors the combination of the Rogers four ratios and the three-ratio technique performed best (Hechifa *et al.*, 2024). Hybrid systems use the conventional methods themselves as inputs or combine their outputs: an integrated scheme of neural networks trained on several DGA techniques (Ghoneim, Taha and Elkalashy, 2016), a random-forest multi-method that integrates existing interpretation methods and is more accurate and consistent than any of them alone (Suwarno *et al.*, 2024), and an intelligent system combining four methods with 89.12 % accuracy against 86.01 % for the best single method (Abdelwahab *et al.*, 2025). Features derived from the coordinates of samples in the Duval Pentagon, Duval Triangle and IEC ratio space have also been used with XGBoost (Li *et al.*, 2026).

### Class Imbalance and Small Data

Imbalanced, insufficient and overlapping data are repeatedly named as a main limitation of DGA classification (Al-Sakini *et al.*, 2024; Adekunle *et al.*, 2025). The synthetic minority over-sampling technique, SMOTE (Chawla *et al.*, 2002), is the most common remedy (Das *et al.*, 2023; Prasojo *et al.*, 2023). Over-sampling must be applied only to training data; otherwise synthetic copies of test records enter training. Class weights, which give each class the same total weight during training without creating new records, are an alternative that avoids this risk. Imputation of missing gas values has also been studied for incomplete data (Wang *et al.*, 2023).

### Explainability

Explanations of ML decisions are needed if engineers are to trust them. SHAP values (Lundberg and Lee, 2017), and their exact form for tree ensembles (Lundberg *et al.*, 2020), distribute each prediction among the input features. In DGA studies, SHAP showed that engineered relative-concentration features and ratios were among the most influential (Doğan *et al.*, 2026), feature importance highlighted hydrogen and ratio-based features (Tanjim *et al.*, 2026), and SHAP was used to rank the contribution of Duval and IEC coordinate features (Li *et al.*, 2026). Permutation importance (Breiman, 2001) is a simpler, model-agnostic measure, but it underestimates the importance of correlated predictors (Strobl *et al.*, 2008), which is the normal situation for gas features derived from the same five gases. Few studies check whether the explanations agree with the known physics of gas formation.

## Validation, Data Leakage and Reproducibility

Data leakage, any route by which test information reaches training or model selection, is a widespread cause of over-optimistic results across ML-based science (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). Common forms include pre-processing fitted on the whole data set, duplicate records split across training and test sets, and the selection of models or settings using test performance. Selecting the settings of a model with the same cross-validation that is used to report its performance biases the estimate upwards; nested cross-validation, in which tuning happens only inside each outer training part, removes this bias (Varma and Simon, 2006). When classifiers are compared on cross-validation folds, the folds are not independent; the corrected resampled t-test allows for this (Nadeau and Bengio, 2003), and rank-based tests are recommended for comparing several classifiers (Demšar, 2006).

These issues are relevant to DGA. Many studies combine records from utilities, the literature and the IEC TC 10 database in one pool (Al-Sakini *et al.*, 2024; Dladla and Thango, 2025b), so the same case may appear more than once, and in some data sets the labels themselves were produced by a combination of conventional methods (Wang, Littler and Liu, 2021). A model trained on labels produced by the Rogers ratio and Duval Triangle can at best reproduce those methods; its accuracy says nothing about real faults. The systematic review of Dladla and Thango (2025a) also found that most studies focus on accuracy, put little effort into other metrics and do not explore regularisation against over-fitting. Released models are rarely documented in a standard way, although structured "model cards" have been proposed for this purpose (Mitchell *et al.*, 2019).

## Online Monitoring and Reduced Gas Sets

Most DGA is still done in laboratories on periodic samples, but online monitors fitted to transformers allow continuous detection of incipient faults (Bustamante *et al.*, 2019). Monitors differ in the number of gases they measure: fault-detection monitors measure only hydrogen and carbon monoxide and can only detect that a fault is present, while fault-diagnosis monitors measure five to nine gases and can identify the fault type (Bustamante *et al.*, 2019). Multi-gas monitors are expensive, so they are often installed only on critical or suspect transformers, and the industry installs cheaper hydrogen monitors across the fleet (Su, Madan and Purushothaman, 2024).

Fewer gases limit the conventional methods directly: the ratio methods need all five gases, and the Duval Triangle needs CH4, C2H4 and C2H2. Studies of ML with incomplete DGA data have imputed missing values; one study reported an average accuracy of 89.5 % with up to 20 % of the values missing (Wang *et al.*, 2023). No study found in this review measured systematically, with a pre-set tolerance and confidence intervals, how much diagnostic performance an ML model loses for every possible subset of the five gases.

## Transformer Thermal Modelling and Simulation

The temperature of the winding hot spot governs the ageing of the paper insulation and therefore the life of a transformer. IEC 60076-7 gives differential equations for the top-oil and hot-spot temperatures as functions of the load (IEC, 2018). Susa, Lehtonen and Nordman (2005) developed dynamic thermal models that account for oil viscosity and validated them with fibre-optic measurements on three large units. Comparisons of thermal models with field data, and improvements of the IEC model using optimised parameters, continue to be published (Aslam *et al.*, 2021; Zhang *et al.*, 2024). These models are used in digital twins and in decisions on dynamic loading. In this thesis the thermal model plays a different role: it provides the operating context (load, temperatures, ageing) next to which a DGA diagnosis is shown, not a source of gas data.

## Integrated Frameworks, Digital Twins and Deployment

Several recent studies move from classification experiments towards systems. A transformer digital twin was combined with an optimised kernel extreme learning machine for online diagnosis (Yao *et al.*, 2024); multi-source data fusion predicted load, oil and winding temperatures and fused gas indices from a monitoring platform (Cui *et al.*, 2025); a probabilistic prognostics framework based on Duval's polygons forecast gas trends from sensor data (Kashiwagi *et al.*, 2025); and a deep-learning asset-management approach classified faults from online DGA data (Jin *et al.*, 2024). Artificial intelligence is increasingly part of power-system asset management in general (Rajora *et al.*, 2024). A recent random-forest framework for transformer fault detection and diagnosis from DGA also stressed interpretability and support for predictive maintenance (Owusu, 2026), and in Ghana a random-forest study of DGA records has been reported as a preprint from the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (Adu-Gyamfi, 2026).

These systems seldom show the conventional diagnosis that engineers already trust next to the ML answer, and they seldom report whether the deployed system returns exactly the same answers as the offline analysis on which its accuracy claims rest. Where an interface is described, its response time is rarely measured.

## Research Gap and Summary

The review shows that conventional DGA methods are trusted but limited by disagreement, no-diagnosis cases and fixed boundaries, and that ML can classify DGA records with high reported accuracy. It also reveals the gaps in @tab:gaps, which this thesis addresses.
