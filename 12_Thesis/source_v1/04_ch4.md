### Gas Concentrations by Class

@tab:gasstats and @fig:gasbox show how the gases differ between the classes of the development set. All five gases differ significantly between classes (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.001), but by very different amounts. ## Performance of the Conventional Methods

Over all {{n_master}} records, the IEC 60599 method gave no diagnosis for {{iec_nd_all}} records ({{iec_nd_all_pct}}) and the Rogers method for {{rogers_nd_all}} ({{rogers_nd_all_pct}}), after the normality gate. The Duval Triangle gave an answer for all but {{duval_nd_all}} record, but {{duval_dt_all}} of its answers fell in the DT zone, which has no matching class. The gate declared {{gate_upper_all}} records Normal. @tab:conventional gives the results on the three primary test sets, and @fig:conf-iec shows the confusion matrices on the IEC TC 10 benchmark.

[[TABLE:conventional]] {#tab:conventional}

Three features stand out. First, the ratio methods leave a large share of records without a diagnosis: their coverage is between {{int_rog_cov}} and {{iec_iecm_cov}}, while the Duval Triangle always answers. Second, conditional accuracy is much higher than strict accuracy for the ratio methods; on the IEC TC 10 benchmark the Rogers method is right on {{iec_rog_cond}} of the records it diagnoses but only {{iec_rog_strict}} of all records. Third, the Duval Triangle is the best conventional method on every test set, and on the IEC TC 10 benchmark its strict accuracy is {{iec_duv_strict}}. The Rogers method has no D1 case, so it cannot diagnose any D1 record (@fig:conf-iec), and on the internal test set both Rogers and Duval gave F1 = 0 for PD.

![Confusion matrices of the conventional methods on the IEC TC 10 power transformers (n = 58): rows are the inspected faults, columns the answers, including no diagnosis (ND) and the Duval DT zone](11_Results/figures/F4_5b_confusion_conventional_iec_tc10.png){#fig:conf-iec width=15.5}

These results carry the home-advantage caveat: the IEC 60599 limits were refined on these same cases (Duval and dePablo, 2001), so the accuracy of the conventional methods on the benchmark may be optimistic.

## Classifier Comparison and Model Selection

### Cross-Validation Results

@tab:cv and @fig:cvbox give the nested cross-validation results. Every classifier was far above the majority baseline (macro-F1 {{cv_F4_majority}}). The engineered feature sets helped: for every classifier, F3 or F4 gave a higher mean than the raw log-gases (F1) or the gas shares (F2). The best pipeline was the random forest on F4 (macro-F1 {{cv_F4_random_forest}} ± {{cv_F4_random_forest_sd}}), followed by XGBoost on F4 ({{cv_F4_xgboost}}) and on F3 ({{cv_F3_xgboost}}). Logistic regression, the simplest model, reached {{cv_F3_logistic_regression}} on F3, less than 0.03 below the best. The per-class results (@app:extra) show that D1 and T2 are the hardest classes (random-forest F1 {{cv_rf_f1_D1}} and {{cv_rf_f1_T2}}) and that T3 and Normal are the easiest.

The Friedman test showed that the six models on F4 differ (χ^2^ = {{friedman_chi2}}, p {{friedman_p}}). With the Nemenyi critical difference of {{nemenyi_cd}} ranks (@fig:cd), the random forest (average rank {{rank_random_forest}}) and XGBoost ({{rank_xgboost}}) form the top group and cannot be told apart, while both are significantly better than logistic regression ({{rank_logistic_regression}}), the SVM ({{rank_svm}}), the MLP ({{rank_mlp}}) and the baseline. ### Selected Model

The written selection rule compared the best pipeline (random forest on F4) with the next (XGBoost on F4) using the corrected resampled t-test: the mean difference was {{sel_diff}}, t = {{sel_t}}, p = {{sel_p}}. The difference was not significant and the random forest is the simpler model family, so the random forest on F4 was selected. Seven other pipelines were not significantly worse than the best (same test, without correction for multiple comparisons), including logistic regression on F3, which shows that the advantage of complex models on these data is small. The final model was tuned on all {{n_dev}} development records (500 trees, no depth limit, minimum leaf size 1) and released as dga_model_v1 before any test record was predicted.

## Agreement and Disagreement between the Methods

### Agreement

@tab:kappa and @fig:kappa show Cohen's kappa between all pairs. The ML model agreed best with the true label on the internal and Li sets (kappa {{k_int_true_ml}} and {{k_li_true_ml}}), but on the IEC TC 10 benchmark the Duval Triangle agreed better with the truth ({{k_iec_true_duv}}) than the ML model ({{k_iec_true_ml}}). The two ratio methods agreed strongly with each other (kappa {{k_int_rog_iecm}} on the internal test set), as expected from methods that use the same three ratios, while their agreement with the ML model was only fair to moderate (for example {{k_int_ml_rog}} and {{k_int_ml_iecm}} on the internal test set). In other words, the ML model and the conventional methods reach their answers in different ways.

## Explaining the Model

At the level of gases (@fig:perm), shuffling C2H4 caused the largest drop in macro-F1 on the internal test set ({{pi_int_C2H4}}), followed by C2H2, H2 and C2H6. On the IEC TC 10 benchmark the order was {{pi_iec_order}}; shuffling C2H6 there even slightly improved the score, which suggests that the model's use of ethane does not transfer well to these cases. The feature-level panel shows that single features have small importance, because the information of each gas is shared by several correlated features.

![Permutation importance of the selected model on the internal test set: (a) gases, (b) single features; bars are 95 % ranges over 30 shuffles](11_Results/figures/F4_14_permutation_importance.png){#fig:perm width=15.5}

The SHAP summary (@fig:shap) shows how each feature pushes each class. The model's reasoning agrees with gas-formation physics: for each of the six fault classes at least one of its three most important features involves an expected key gas ({{eng_consistent}} classes). High shares of C2H2 push towards D1 and D2, a low CH4/H2 ratio and a high hydrogen share push towards PD, and a high C2H4/C2H6 ratio pushes towards T3. The ranking of the features on the test records agreed closely with that on the development records (Spearman correlation {{shap_spearman}}).

## Exploratory Improvement Study

The confirmatory results showed that the released model did not beat the best conventional methods on the IEC TC 10 benchmark. Study 2 explored, under a pre-registered protocol, whether pooling the sources or adding the conventional answers as inputs would help. Its results are exploratory because the IEC TC 10 and Li sets had already been seen.

[[TABLE:study2]] {#tab:study2}

@tab:study2 and @fig:s2f1 show the leave-one-source-out results. Retraining on the pooled sources (model B) and the hybrid model (C) gave higher point estimates than the released model on every external source; on the IEC TC 10 power transformers the hybrid model reached macro-F1 {{s2_IECTC10_C_f1}} against {{s2_IECTC10_A_f1}} for the released model. However, neither pre-registered decision rule was met: the improvement of B over A had a confidence interval that included zero on all three external sources, and C was not significantly better than B on any source (on the Dissanayake training source it was worse). Pooling and the hybrid design are therefore not shown to help.

![Study 2: macro-F1 with 95 % intervals for each held-out source](11_Results/figures/F4_23_study2_macro_f1.png){#fig:s2f1 width=15.5}

The reject option was more encouraging (@tab:reject; @fig:s2rc). When the hybrid model was allowed to abstain on its least confident records until its coverage equalled that of IEC 60599, it was more accurate on the records it answered than IEC 60599 on the records IEC 60599 answered, on every source; the confidence interval of the difference excluded zero on all sources except the IEC TC 10 power transformers ({{s2r_IECTC10_IEC60599_sel}} against {{s2r_IECTC10_IEC60599_cond}}, difference interval {{s2r_IECTC10_IEC60599_ci}}). This suggests a practical way of using ML next to the conventional methods: the model answers when it is confident and refers the remaining samples to an expert, just as a ratio method gives no diagnosis. Confirming this needs fresh inspection-confirmed data, which a search of legitimate public sources did not find.

## Discussion

### Comparison with the Literature

The cross-validation macro-F1 of {{cv_F4_random_forest}} and the internal-test accuracy of {{int_ml_strict}} are lower than the accuracies above 95 % reported by many studies (Das *et al.*, 2023; Hechifa *et al.*, 2024; Adekunle *et al.*, 2025). Three features of this study explain much of the difference. The primary measure, macro-F1 over seven classes, gives the small and difficult classes (D1, T2) the same weight as the easy ones, whereas accuracy rewards the majority classes. Leakage was removed: {{n_removed_leak}} training records that matched test or benchmark cases were deleted, near-duplicates were kept in one fold, and the test sets were opened only once. Finally, the main benchmark consists of inspected faults, not labels produced by a method. Studies that test on independent data or under a strict protocol also report lower figures (Hechifa *et al.*, 2024; Nkwambe and Thango, 2026), which is consistent with the warning of Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) that leakage inflates reported performance.

The selection of a random forest, with XGBoost statistically indistinguishable, agrees with the literature in which tree ensembles perform best on DGA data (Rao *et al.*, 2021; Adekunle *et al.*, 2025; Doğan *et al.*, 2026). That logistic regression on gas ratios came within about 0.03 of the best model is less often reported; it supports the finding of Chen and Zhang (2025) that more complex models add little on DGA data, and it shows the value of including a simple baseline. The benefit of engineered ratio features agrees with Hechifa *et al.* (2024) and Taha, Ibrahim and Mansour (2021).

The strong performance of the Duval Triangle on the IEC TC 10 benchmark differs from studies that report very low Duval accuracy on pooled data, such as 35.8 % (Dladla and Thango, 2025b). Two explanations are likely. The IEC TC 10 cases are the kind of clear, inspected faults on which the conventional methods were built and refined, so the methods enjoy a home advantage. In addition, the merged T1/T2 class and the five-class scoring of the benchmark remove the finest thermal distinctions, where the triangle is weakest. On the internal and Li sets, whose labels are of unknown origin, the conventional methods did much worse, and the ML model was significantly better than all of them. Which of these pictures is closer to field practice can only be decided with new inspection-confirmed data.

### Practical Implications

Four implications follow for utility engineers. First, ML should complement, not replace, the conventional methods: on inspection-confirmed faults the best conventional method (Duval) was at least as accurate, but ML gave an answer for every record, and its errors were partly different from those of the conventional methods (kappa between ML and the methods was only fair to moderate). Showing both answers side by side, as the dashboard does, lets the engineer see when they disagree and treat such samples with care. Second, the reject option of Study 2 suggests a safe way to use ML: answer when confident and refer the rest to an expert. Third, a monitor that measures H2, C2H6, C2H4 and C2H2 kept almost full ML performance, a combination that no conventional method can interpret, while hydrogen alone was far from sufficient for diagnosis. Fourth, the framework runs fast enough for interactive use on an ordinary laptop and reproduced the offline results exactly. This is a necessary condition for trusting a deployed model and is rarely checked in the literature.

### Limitations

The results are limited by the data. Only the IEC TC 10 cases have inspection-confirmed labels, and among its 58 power transformers there is only one PD case and eight T1/T2 cases, so per-class results on the benchmark have wide intervals. The labels of the Dissanayake and Li sets are of unknown origin, and the Dissanayake training file was shown to contain relabelled IEC TC 10 cases. The conventional methods had a home advantage on the benchmark. CO and CO2, which indicate paper involvement, could not be used. The full texts of some standards were not available, so the limits come from official previews, the 2008 IEEE guide and peer-reviewed secondary sources. The Simulink model represents a typical 40 MVA unit with default thermal constants and illustrative scenarios, and the framework was tested on a single laptop. Study 2 is exploratory and needs confirmation on fresh data.
