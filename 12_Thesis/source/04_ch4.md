# Data Collection and Preparation {#sec:ch4}

## Introduction

The value of any comparison between diagnostic methods depends on the data used. This chapter describes where the DGA records came from and how the origin of their fault labels was established (@sec:sources), how they were audited, cleaned and divided into a development set and three test sets (@sec:prep and @sec:evalsets), the main threats to validity that the data carry (@sec:threats), and what the cleaned data look like (@sec:describe).

## Data Sources {#sec:sources}

### Selection of the Data Sets

Data sets were sought that contain measured concentrations of the five gases H2, CH4, C2H6, C2H4 and C2H2 together with the fault found in each transformer, and that are published by a legitimate source with known terms of use. Four data sets met these requirements (@tab:datasets).

[[TABLE:datasets]] {#tab:datasets}

- **IEC TC 10 database.** Duval and dePablo (2001) published, in an annex to their paper, the DGA results of faulty equipment that was inspected in service, together with the fault found at the inspection. The {{n_raw_iec}} cases were transcribed from the paper and checked cell by cell. Because the labels come from inspection and not from an interpretation method, this database is the main benchmark of the study. The equipment includes power transformers (codes P and U), instrument transformers and other apparatus; the {{n_iec}} power transformers form the primary benchmark.
- **Dissanayake (2026) data set.** This data set on IEEE DataPort contains a training file ({{n_raw_distrain}} records) and an unseen test file ({{n_raw_distest}} records, ten of each of seven classes), with fault classes coded 0 to 6 (Normal, PD, D1, D2, T1, T2, T3). A third file ({{n_raw_disiec}} records) is a subset of the IEC TC 10 database. The way the labels were obtained is not documented.
- **Li (2019) data set.** This data set on IEEE DataPort contains {{n_raw_li}} records with text labels such as "arc discharge" and "high-temperature overheating". The way the labels were obtained is not documented.

All files were stored unchanged, and their SHA-256 fingerprints were recorded so that any later change would be detected.

### Origin of the Fault Labels

The origin of the labels decided the role of each data set. A model trained on labels produced by a conventional method can at best imitate that method, and a method cannot be judged fairly on labels that it produced. The audit established three facts:

- the IEC TC 10 labels were obtained by visual inspection of the failed equipment, independently of any interpretation method;
- the Dissanayake training file contained 96 cases of the IEC TC 10 database, and for the combined T1/T2 cases among them it gives specific labels (T1, T2, T3 or PD) that the inspection does not support, so at least part of its labelling is not based on inspection; and
- the Li (2019) file contains 115 of the 117 IEC TC 10 cases, with the inspection labels, and 86 other records.

The data sets were therefore given the roles shown in the last column of @tab:datasets. The Dissanayake training file was used for development only, its unseen file served as an internal test set of supporting evidence, the IEC TC 10 database was the main benchmark, and the Li (2019) set was used as an external sensitivity test.

## Data Preparation {#sec:prep}

### Standardisation

Each source was converted to one common table with a record identifier, the five gas concentrations in parts per million (ppm), the fault class and the data role. Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide were not used, because only the IEC TC 10 database reports them. The fault classes were coded 0 to 6 as Normal, PD, D1, D2, T1, T2 and T3. The IEC TC 10 annex prints the thermal faults below 700 °C in one table, so these cases form a merged class T1/T2. The Li (2019) text labels were mapped as follows: partial discharge to PD, spark discharge to D1, arc discharge to D2, low-, middle- and high-temperature overheating to T1, T2 and T3, and low/middle-temperature overheating to T1/T2.

### Values Not Available

Some values are missing in the sources: the IEC TC 10 annex prints a dash, and the Dissanayake files use the placeholder 0.0001 ppm. The pattern of the missing values was examined before a rule was chosen. Acetylene was missing in most T1 records and never in D1 or D2 records, which is what would be expected if the gas had simply not been detected: low-temperature thermal faults produce little or no acetylene, whereas discharges always do. Dropping incomplete records would have removed almost all T1 cases. A value that is not available was therefore treated as **not detected** and set to 0 ppm, and flags recording the original form of each value were kept with the record. Results on complete records only were computed as a sensitivity analysis.

### Removing Duplicates and Leakage

Data leakage occurs when a test record, or a copy of it, is also used for training; it makes the measured performance optimistic (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). Three kinds of problem were found and corrected in the development file:

- **{{n_removed_leak}} records** that copy a record of a test set or of the benchmark, exactly or within 1 % or 1 ppm in every gas, were removed;
- **{{n_removed_copy}} exact copies** within the file were removed; and
- **{{n_removed_conflict}} records** with identical gases but conflicting labels were removed, because no method can be right on both.

The 49-record IEC subset file of the Dissanayake data set was not used; the full transcription of the IEC TC 10 database replaces it.

### Overlap between the Test Sets

The Li (2019) set contains 115 of the IEC TC 10 cases, and the Dissanayake unseen file contains 9 of them. A test set reported as separate evidence must not share records with another, so test records that copy a benchmark case were flagged. Results of the internal and external test sets use only their independent records ({{n_int}} of {{n_int_all}} and {{n_li}} of {{n_li_all}} records); the full sets were evaluated as a sensitivity analysis. No record was deleted from a test set.

### Near-Duplicate Groups and Cross-Validation Folds

Records whose five gas concentrations agree within 1 % or 1 ppm were placed in one group, and a group was never split between training and validation folds. The {{n_dev}} development records were divided into 5 folds of similar class composition, repeated 10 times with different random orders, giving the 50 outer folds of the nested cross-validation described in @sec:ch3.

## Evaluation Sets {#sec:evalsets}

Three primary evaluation sets were fixed before any test record was predicted:

- **Internal test set:** {{n_int}} Dissanayake unseen records independent of the benchmark, with seven classes (Normal, PD, D1, D2, T1, T2, T3).
- **IEC TC 10 benchmark (main test set):** the {{n_iec}} power transformers, scored on five merged classes (PD, D1, D2, T1/T2, T3). A prediction of T1 or T2 counts as T1/T2, and a Normal prediction counts as wrong, because every case is a confirmed fault. All {{n_iec_all}} cases form a sensitivity analysis.
- **External test set:** {{n_li}} Li (2019) records independent of the benchmark, with six fault classes.

Together these {{n_primary_test}} records are the test records referred to in Chapters 5 and 6.

## Threats to Validity {#sec:threats}

Three threats to validity were identified in advance and are kept in view when the results are interpreted:

- **Home advantage of the conventional methods.** The IEC 60599 ratio limits were refined using the IEC TC 10 cases (Duval and dePablo, 2001), so the conventional methods may perform better on the main benchmark than they would on other data.
- **Labels of unknown origin.** The labels of the Dissanayake and Li data sets are not documented; results on them are supporting evidence only.
- **Small classes.** The {{n_iec}} benchmark power transformers include only one PD case and eight T1/T2 cases, so per-class results on the benchmark carry wide confidence intervals.

## Description of the Data {#sec:describe}

### Records per Class

@tab:records and @fig:classes give the number of records per class in each data role. The development set contains all seven classes ({{dev_class_counts}}), and its largest class has about twice as many records as its smallest. The internal test set is balanced by design. Among the IEC TC 10 power transformers, high-energy discharges (D2) dominate and partial discharge has a single case. Because of this imbalance, macro-F1 is the primary measure and every result carries a confidence interval.

### Gas Concentrations by Class

@tab:gasstats and @fig:gasbox show how the gases differ between the classes of the development set. All five gases differ significantly between the classes (Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.001), but by very different amounts. Acetylene separates the classes most strongly (rank epsilon-squared {{kw_eps_C2H2}}), followed by ethylene ({{kw_eps_C2H4}}), ethane ({{kw_eps_C2H6}}), hydrogen ({{kw_eps_H2}}) and methane ({{kw_eps_CH4}}).

[[TABLE:gas_stats]] {#tab:gasstats}

![Gas concentrations by fault class in the development set](12_Thesis/figures/thesis/gas_boxplots.png){#fig:gasbox width=15.5}

The pattern agrees with the physics of gas formation reviewed in @sec:ch2:

- acetylene has a median of zero in every class except D1 and D2, the discharge classes, where it reaches tens to hundreds of ppm;
- partial discharge has the highest hydrogen of all classes;
- ethylene rises from T1 through T2 to T3 as the fault temperature increases, while ethane is highest in T1; and
- the Normal records have the lowest concentration of every gas.

The same pattern appears in the IEC TC 10 benchmark (Appendix B).

### Correlation between the Gases

The gases are correlated with one another (@fig:gascorr). The three hydrocarbons CH4, C2H6 and C2H4 are the most strongly related (Spearman correlation up to 0.74), because thermal decomposition of the oil produces them together, whereas acetylene is almost unrelated to ethane. Correlated gases carry overlapping information; this matters for the explanation of the model (Chapter 5) and for the reduced-gas study (Chapter 7).

![Spearman rank correlation between the five gases, development set (n = 439)](12_Thesis/figures/thesis/gas_correlation.png){#fig:gascorr width=10.5}

### The Benchmark in the Duval Triangle

@fig:duval-iec places the {{n_iec_all}} IEC TC 10 cases in the Duval Triangle 1. Most cases lie in the zone of their inspected fault: the discharges in the D1 and D2 zones, the high-temperature thermal faults in T3 and the lower-temperature thermal faults along the right-hand edge. Many points, however, lie close to a zone boundary, especially between D1 and D2 and between the thermal zones; for such points a small change in one gas would change the diagnosis. The triangles of the other data sets are given in Appendix B.

## Summary

Four published data sets were audited. Only the IEC TC 10 database has fault labels confirmed by inspection, and it became the main benchmark. The audit removed {{n_removed_leak}} development records that leaked test cases, together with exact copies and conflicting records, and flagged test records that copy benchmark cases. Values that were not available were treated as not detected. The cleaned master table of {{n_master}} records was divided into a development set of {{n_dev}} records and three primary test sets of {{n_int}}, {{n_iec}} and {{n_li}} records, and frozen. The gas concentrations differ between the fault classes in the way gas-formation physics predicts, with acetylene and ethylene the most informative gases.
