# Methods Used {#sec:ch3}

## Introduction

This chapter describes the methods used in the research: the overall research design, the conventional DGA interpretation methods, the machine-learning methods and their validation, the measures and statistical tests, the reduced-gas study, the thermal model of the transformer and the methods used to integrate and evaluate the framework. The data to which the methods were applied are described in Chapter 4, and the results are presented in Chapters 5 to 8. Every equation is followed by the meaning and unit of each of its symbols.

## Research Design

### Overview

The research is a quantitative, comparative study carried out on published, measured data and on computer models; no equipment was built or tested in a laboratory. @fig:framework shows the framework. It has three parts:

- **Measured data.** Dissolved-gas records of transformers whose fault is known are collected from published sources, audited and cleaned (Chapter 4).
- **Offline analysis in Python.** Machine-learning classifiers are trained and compared, one model is selected and tested, and its diagnoses are compared statistically with those of the conventional methods (Chapters 5 to 7).
- **Demonstration test bench in MATLAB and Simulink.** The conventional methods run in MATLAB; a Simulink model of a transformer provides the operating context; a dashboard shows the conventional and ML diagnoses side by side; and the released ML model is reached through a web service (Chapter 8).

![The integrated framework: measured data, Python analysis and service, and the MATLAB/Simulink test bench](12_Thesis/figures/F3_1_framework.png){#fig:framework width=15.5}

### Principles that Governed the Work

Four principles were followed throughout, because they decide whether the results can be trusted:

- **Only measured gas values.** Every gas concentration used is a published measurement. The Simulink model represents load, temperature and insulation ageing only and never generates a gas value, because a gas-generation model without laboratory validation would amount to inventing data.
- **Labels of known origin.** A fault label obtained by a conventional method cannot be used to judge that method. The main comparison therefore uses only cases whose fault was confirmed by inspection of the failed equipment.
- **Analysis rules fixed in advance.** The measures, tests, tolerances and decision criteria were written in an analysis plan and approved before any result was computed, and every detail that the plan left open was fixed and recorded before the corresponding computation. In particular, the final model was chosen by a written rule before any test record was seen, and the test records were predicted only once.
- **Traceability.** Every number in this thesis is produced by a program from saved files, and every data file is identified by its SHA-256 fingerprint (NIST, 2015), so that the whole analysis can be repeated.

### Data Roles

Four groups of records with different roles were used (Chapter 4). The **development set** is used to train, tune and compare the classifiers by cross-validation. Three **test sets** are kept closed until the final model has been chosen and are then predicted once: an **internal test set** from the same source as the development set; the **IEC TC 10 benchmark**, the main test set, whose faults were confirmed by inspection; and an **external test set** from a different source, used as a sensitivity check.

## Conventional DGA Interpretation Methods

Three conventional methods were implemented: the Rogers ratio method, the IEC 60599 ratio method and the Duval Triangle 1. They were chosen because they are the most widely used ratio and graphical methods and because their limits are published. The limits were transcribed from the sources given under each table and checked against the worked examples of those sources; no limit is typed into the program code.

### Gas Ratios

The two ratio methods use three ratios of gas concentrations:

$$R_1=(CH_4)/(H_2),  R_2=(C_2 H_2)/(C_2 H_4),  R_5=(C_2 H_4)/(C_2 H_6)$$ {#eq:ratios}

where:
: *R*~1~, *R*~2~, *R*~5~ = gas ratios (dimensionless), named as in IEEE Std C57.104
: H2, CH4, C2H2, C2H4, C2H6 = concentrations of hydrogen, methane, acetylene, ethylene and ethane in the oil (ppm)

A ratio whose denominator is zero is undefined. A method gives **no diagnosis (ND)** for a record when a ratio that the candidate fault class needs is undefined, or when the ratios match none of the coded classes. Ratios that the standard marks "not significant" for a class are not used for that class.

### IEC 60599 Ratio Method {#sec:m-iec}

@tab:iec gives the limits of IEC 60599. A record receives a fault class when every significant ratio of that class falls within the printed range. Three implementation rules were needed:

- Ranges printed as "a – b" include both end points; "<" and ">" are strict.
- The D1 and D2 rows overlap. Where a record satisfies both, the method returns the dual answer "D1/D2", as the standard allows; it is counted as correct when the true class is D1 or D2.
- The T1 entry for CH4/H2 is printed "> 1 but NS". Read only as "not significant", every partial-discharge record would also satisfy T1 and the method could never answer PD alone. The printed condition CH4/H2 > 1 is therefore applied.

### Duval Triangle 1

The Duval Triangle 1 uses only methane, ethylene and acetylene. Their shares of the three-gas total are

$$%CH_4=100 (CH_4)/(CH_4+C_2 H_4+C_2 H_2)$$ {#eq:duvalpct}

where:
: %CH4 = share of methane in the total of the three gases (%); %C2H4 and %C2H2 are defined in the same way, and the three shares add up to 100 %

Because the three shares add up to 100 %, each record is one point of an equilateral triangle. With the triangle drawn with side 1, the point has the coordinates

$$x=(%C_2 H_4+0.5 %CH_4)/100,  y=(√3/2)(%CH_4/100)$$ {#eq:duvalxy}

where:
: *x*, *y* = coordinates of the record in the triangle; *x* = 0 at the 100 % C2H2 corner and 1 at the 100 % C2H4 corner; *y* = √3/2 at the 100 % CH4 corner

The triangle is divided into seven zones by the rules of @tab:duval, which are tested in order; the first rule that holds gives the zone. The rules were taken from a secondary source that cites the zone limits of IEEE Std C57.104-2019 (Moser, 2025) and checked against the zone labels of an open-access figure (Manisha *et al.*, 2022). Together they cover the triangle without gaps or overlaps and reproduce all 39 example diagnoses printed in those sources. The DT zone (a mixture of thermal and electrical faults) has no matching fault class in the data, so a DT answer is counted as a wrong diagnosis and reported separately.

[[TABLE:duval_table]] {#tab:duval}

### Normality Gate

Conventional methods cannot by themselves say that a transformer is healthy, and gas ratios of equipment with low gas levels have no diagnostic meaning (Duval and dePablo, 2001). A common normality gate based on the 90 % typical concentrations of IEC 60599 (@tab:typical) was therefore applied in front of all three methods:

$$y="Normal"  "if"  x_g≤x_g^"typ"  "for every gas " g$$ {#eq:gate}

where:
: *y* = answer of the conventional method
: *x*~g~ = measured concentration of gas *g* (ppm)
: *x*~g~^typ^ = upper end of the 90 % typical range of gas *g* for the equipment type (ppm)

The upper end of each range is the primary rule; the lower end, and no gate at all, were run as sensitivity analyses.

[[TABLE:typical_table]] {#tab:typical}

### Coverage and Distance to the Decision Boundary

The share of records for which a method gives no diagnosis, and its complement, the coverage, are

$$U=n_"ND"/N,  "Coverage"=1-U$$ {#eq:coverage}

where:
: *U* = unresolved (no-diagnosis) rate; *n*~ND~ = number of records without a diagnosis; *N* = number of records

To test whether ML and the conventional methods disagree mainly near the decision boundaries of the conventional methods, the distance of each record to the nearest boundary was computed. For the ratio methods it is measured in decades of the ratio:

$$d_R=min┬(j,l) |log_10⁡ R_j-log_10⁡ L_(j,l) |$$ {#eq:dist-ratio}

where:
: *d*~R~ = distance to the nearest ratio limit (decades)
: *R*~j~ = value of ratio *j* of the record; *L*~j,l~ = the *l*-th limit of ratio *j* in the code table

For the Duval Triangle it is the shortest Euclidean distance, in percentage points, from the record's point *P* to an edge that separates two different zones:

$$d_D=min┬(e∈E) "dist"(P,e)$$ {#eq:dist-duval}

where:
: *d*~D~ = distance to the nearest zone boundary (percentage points)
: *E* = set of zone edges that separate two different zones; dist(*P*, *e*) = shortest distance from *P* to edge *e*

### Verification of the Implementation

The MATLAB functions were checked by 67 unit tests and against the worked examples of the sources. The three methods were then programmed a second time, independently, in Python from the same tables and rules, with the Duval distance computed by a different geometric procedure. The two implementations were compared on every record (@sec:conv-verification).

## Machine-Learning Methods

### Overview

A supervised classifier learns, from records whose class is known, a function that maps the five gas concentrations of a sample to one of the fault classes, and returns for each sample the probability of every class. The method has five parts: the input features, the classifiers, the handling of class imbalance, the validation design and the selection rule. All were implemented in Python with scikit-learn (Pedregosa *et al.*, 2011).

### Input Features

Gas concentrations are strongly skewed: most records contain a few to a few hundred ppm of each gas, but some contain tens of thousands. Four feature sets were therefore built from the five gases (@tab:features):

$$u_g=log_10⁡(x_g+1)$$ {#eq:loggas}

$$p_g=100 x_g/(∑_(h=1)^5 x_h)$$ {#eq:pctgas}

$$r_k=log_10⁡((a_k+1)/(b_k+1))$$ {#eq:logratio}

where:
: *u*~g~ = log-transformed concentration of gas *g*; the offset of 1 ppm keeps a value of zero finite
: *p*~g~ = share of gas *g* in the total of the five gases (%)
: *r*~k~ = log-ratio *k* of the gases *a*~k~ and *b*~k~: R1 = CH4/H2, R2 = C2H2/C2H4, R3 = C2H2/CH4, R4 = C2H6/C2H2 and R5 = C2H4/C2H6

[[TABLE:feature_sets]] {#tab:features}

Before training, every feature was standardised with the mean and standard deviation of the training records:

$$z=(x-μ)/σ$$ {#eq:zscore}

where:
: *z* = standardised feature; *x* = feature value; *μ*, *σ* = mean and standard deviation of the feature over the training records

The Duval percentages are undefined for records in which CH4, C2H4 and C2H2 are all zero; such values were replaced by the median of the training records. Every transformation was fitted on the training part only and then applied unchanged to the records being predicted, so that no information from those records could reach the model.

### Classifiers

Five classifiers of different families were compared, with a majority-class baseline that always predicts the most common class as a floor (@tab:grids). In the descriptions below, *z* is the vector of standardised features of a record and *K* the number of classes.

#### Multinomial logistic regression

Logistic regression is a linear model and serves as an interpretable baseline. The probability of class *c* is

$$P(c|z)=e^(w_c^⊤ z+b_c)/(∑_(k=1)^K e^(w_k^⊤ z+b_k))$$ {#eq:logreg}

where:
: *w*~c~, *b*~c~ = weight vector and intercept of class *c*, estimated by maximising the penalised likelihood of the training data; the strength of the L2 penalty is set by the parameter *C* (smaller *C* = stronger penalty)

#### Support vector machine

A support vector machine (SVM) separates two classes by the boundary with the largest margin. With the radial basis function (RBF) kernel, its decision function is

$$f(z)=∑_(i=1)^n α_i y_i K(z_i,z)+b,  K(z_i,z)=e^(-γ‖z_i-z‖^2)$$ {#eq:svm}

where:
: *α*~i~ = weights found in training (non-zero only for the support vectors); *y*~i~ = ±1 = class of training record *i*; *b* = intercept
: *γ* = width parameter of the kernel; the penalty *C* bounds the *α*~i~ and so balances the margin against training errors

Several classes are handled by one-against-one voting. Class probabilities were obtained by Platt scaling (Platt, 1999), which fits a sigmoid function to the decision values inside the training data.

#### Random forest

A random forest (Breiman, 2001) averages many decision trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample of the training records and choosing, at every split, the best of a random subset of the features. A split is chosen to reduce the Gini impurity of the two resulting nodes, and the forest's probability is the mean over the trees:

$$G=1-∑_(c=1)^K p_c^2,  P(c|z)=1/T ∑_(t=1)^T p_(t,c)(z)$$ {#eq:rf}

where:
: *G* = Gini impurity of a node; *p*~c~ = share of training records of class *c* in the node
: *T* = number of trees (500); *p*~t,c~(*z*) = share of class *c* in the leaf of tree *t* that the record reaches

The settings tuned were the maximum depth of the trees and the minimum number of records in a leaf.

#### Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost)

XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) builds trees one after another, each correcting the errors of those before it, by minimising

$$ℒ=∑_(i=1)^n l(y_i,ŷ_i )+∑_(m=1)^M (γ_T T_m+1/2 λ‖w_m ‖^2 )$$ {#eq:xgb}

where:
: *l* = multi-class logarithmic loss between the true class *y*~i~ and the prediction *ŷ*~i~, which is the sum of the outputs of the *M* trees
: *T*~m~ = number of leaves of tree *m*; *w*~m~ = its leaf weights; *γ*~T~, *λ* = penalties that keep the trees simple
: the learning rate shrinks the contribution of each new tree; the number of trees, their depth and the learning rate were tuned

#### Multilayer perceptron

The multilayer perceptron (MLP) is a feed-forward neural network. With one hidden layer,

$$h=max(0,W_1 z+b_1 ),  P(c|z)="softmax"(W_2 h+b_2 )_c$$ {#eq:mlp}

where:
: *h* = outputs of the hidden neurons (rectified linear activation); *W*~1~, *W*~2~, *b*~1~, *b*~2~ = weights and biases
: softmax = the normalised exponential of @eq:logreg; the weights are found with the L-BFGS optimiser (Liu and Nocedal, 1989) with an L2 penalty

### Class Imbalance

The fault classes are of unequal size. To stop a classifier from favouring the large classes, every training record received a class weight

$$w_c=N/(K n_c)$$ {#eq:weights}

where:
: *w*~c~ = weight of every training record of class *c*
: *N* = number of training records; *K* = number of classes; *n*~c~ = number of training records of class *c*

With these weights every class carries the same total weight in training. The weights were recomputed from the training records of every fold. Synthetic over-sampling (SMOTE; Chawla *et al.*, 2002) was not used, because synthetic copies can leak test information when they are not confined to the training part.

### Validation Design: Nested Cross-Validation

Each of the 24 combinations of classifier and feature set was evaluated by nested cross-validation (Varma and Simon, 2006) on the development set (@fig:nestedcv):

- **Outer loop.** The development records were divided into 5 folds of similar class composition; each fold served once as the test fold while the other four trained the model. The division was repeated 10 times with different random orders, giving 50 test-fold estimates per pipeline. Records that are near-duplicates of each other were always placed in the same fold, so that a copy of a test record could never be in the training part.
- **Inner loop.** Inside each outer training part, a 5-fold grid search chose the settings of the classifier by macro-F1 (@eq:macro). The chosen setting was refitted on the whole outer training part and scored on the outer test fold, which therefore never influenced any choice.

![Nested cross-validation scheme](12_Thesis/figures/F3_3_nested_cv.png){#fig:nestedcv width=15}

### Model Selection Rule

The rule for choosing the final model was written down before any cross-validation result was read. The pipeline (classifier and feature set) with the highest mean macro-F1 is compared with the second best by the corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau and Bengio, 2003), which allows for the overlap of training data between folds:

$$t=d̄/√((1/(k r)+n_"test"/n_"train") σ_d^2)$$ {#eq:cr-ttest}

where:
: *d̄* = mean over the 50 folds of the difference in macro-F1 between the two pipelines; *σ*~d~^2^ = variance of those differences
: *k* = 5 folds; *r* = 10 repeats; *n*~test~/*n*~train~ = ratio of test-fold to training-part size ({{sel_ratio}}); *t* is compared with a *t* distribution with 49 degrees of freedom

If the difference is not significant at the 5 % level, the simpler pipeline is chosen: first by model family (in the order logistic regression, SVM, random forest, XGBoost, MLP), then by the smaller number of features.

### Final Model and One-Time Testing

The selected pipeline was tuned once more on all development records with the same grid and inner procedure, refitted, and saved with its SHA-256 fingerprint before any test record was predicted. All test records were then predicted in a single run. The program records every access to the test sets and refuses to run a second time, so no choice could be changed after the test results were seen. The saved model was released unchanged for the diagnosis service and documented in a model card (Mitchell *et al.*, 2019).

### Explaining the Model {#sec:m-explain}

Two explanation methods were applied to the final model. **Permutation importance** (Breiman, 2001) measures how much the macro-F1 falls when the values of one input are shuffled at random:

$$I_j=s-1/R ∑_(r=1)^R s_(r,j)$$ {#eq:perm}

where:
: *I*~j~ = importance of input *j*; *s* = macro-F1 on the original data; *s*~r,j~ = macro-F1 after shuffling input *j* in repeat *r*; *R* = 30 repeats

Because the 18 features are derived from only five gases and are strongly correlated, shuffling one feature understates information that other features share (Strobl *et al.*, 2008). The main ranking therefore shuffles whole gases, so that every feature derived from a gas moves with it. **SHAP values** (Lundberg and Lee, 2017), computed exactly for tree models (Lundberg *et al.*, 2020), split each predicted probability into the contributions of the individual features; the mean absolute SHAP value ranks the features for each class. Before the explanations were computed, the gases expected from gas-formation physics were written down for each fault (H2 for PD; C2H2 for D1 and D2; CH4 or C2H6 for T1; C2H4 or CH4 for T2; C2H4 for T3), and it was then checked whether the most important features agree with them.

## Performance Measures

All measures are computed from the confusion matrix, which counts, for every true class, how many records received each diagnosis. For class *c*:

$$"Prec"_c=TP_c/(TP_c+FP_c),  "Rec"_c=TP_c/(TP_c+FN_c),  "F1"_c=(2 "Prec"_c "Rec"_c)/("Prec"_c+"Rec"_c)$$ {#eq:prf}

where:
: Prec~c~, Rec~c~, F1~c~ = precision, recall and F1-score of class *c*
: *TP*~c~ = records of class *c* diagnosed as *c*; *FP*~c~ = records of other classes diagnosed as *c*; *FN*~c~ = records of class *c* given another answer or no diagnosis

The **primary measure** is macro-F1, the unweighted mean of the class F1-scores, because it gives every fault class the same importance however few records it has:

$$"Macro-F1"=1/K ∑_(c=1)^K "F1"_c,  "Balanced accuracy"=1/K ∑_(c=1)^K "Rec"_c$$ {#eq:macro}

where:
: *K* = number of classes in the evaluation set

Because conventional methods may give no diagnosis, two accuracies are reported together with the coverage (@eq:coverage):

$$A_"strict"=n_"correct"/N,  A_"cond"=n_"correct"/(N-n_"ND")$$ {#eq:accuracy}

where:
: *A*~strict~ = strict accuracy, in which every no-diagnosis counts as wrong; *A*~cond~ = conditional accuracy over the records that received a diagnosis; *n*~correct~ = number of correct diagnoses

Agreement between two raters (two methods, or a method and the true class) beyond chance was measured with Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960):

$$κ=(p_o-p_e)/(1-p_e)$$ {#eq:kappa}

where:
: *p*~o~ = observed proportion of records on which the two raters give the same answer; *p*~e~ = proportion of agreement expected by chance from the frequency of each answer

The multi-class Matthews correlation coefficient (Gorodkin, 2004) was also reported. The same scoring rules apply to every method: a no-diagnosis is wrong in strict accuracy and left out of conditional accuracy; the IEC dual answer D1/D2 is correct for a true D1 or D2; a DT answer is wrong; and on test sets without a Normal class a Normal answer is wrong.

## Statistical Methods

All tests are two-sided at the 5 % significance level. Exact p-values and effect sizes are reported, and a non-significant result is described as no evidence of a difference, never as proof that two methods are equal.

### Describing the Data

Gas concentrations by class are summarised by the median and the interquartile range. Whether a gas differs between classes was tested with the Kruskal–Wallis test (Kruskal and Wallis, 1952), a rank test that does not assume normal distributions:

$$H=12/(n(n+1)) ∑_(c=1)^K R_c^2/n_c-3(n+1),  ε_R^2=H(n+1)/(n^2-1)$$ {#eq:kw}

where:
: *H* = Kruskal–Wallis statistic, compared with a χ^2^ distribution with *K* − 1 degrees of freedom; *n* = number of records; *R*~c~ = sum of the ranks of the records of class *c*; *n*~c~ = number of records of class *c*
: *ε*~R~^2^ = rank epsilon-squared effect size, the share of the rank variation explained by the class (0 to 1)

The association between pairs of gases was measured with Spearman's rank correlation *ρ*~s~ (Spearman, 1904).

### Confidence Intervals

Proportions such as accuracy and coverage carry Wilson score intervals (Wilson, 1927), which behave well for small samples:

$$"CI"_95=(p+z^2/(2n)±z√(p(1-p)/n+z^2/(4n^2)))/(1+z^2/n)$$ {#eq:wilson}

where:
: *p* = observed proportion; *n* = number of records; *z* = 1.96

Macro-F1 and the differences between methods carry stratified percentile bootstrap intervals (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993): *B* = 2 000 resamples are drawn with replacement within each true class, the measure is recomputed on each, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles form the interval. The same resamples are used for all methods, so that differences are paired.

### Comparing the Classifiers

In each of the 50 outer folds the six models were ranked by macro-F1, and the Friedman test (Friedman, 1937) checked whether their average ranks differ. Pairs of models were then compared with the Nemenyi critical difference (Demšar, 2006):

$$"CD"=q_α √(k(k+1)/(6 n_f))$$ {#eq:cd}

where:
: *CD* = smallest difference of average ranks that is significant; *q*~α~ = critical value of the studentised range for *k* models at *α* = 0.05, divided by √2; *n*~f~ = 50 folds

Because the folds share training data, these rank tests are descriptive support; the key pair of pipelines was compared with the corrected t-test of @eq:cr-ttest.

### Comparing the ML Model with the Conventional Methods

On each test set every method is either right or wrong on each record. Cochran's Q test (Cochran, 1950) first tests whether the four methods have the same proportion correct:

$$Q=((m-1)(m∑_(j=1)^m C_j^2-T^2))/(m T-∑_(i=1)^N a_i^2)$$ {#eq:cochran}

where:
: *m* = 4 methods; *C*~j~ = number of records method *j* diagnosed correctly; *a*~i~ = number of methods correct on record *i*; *T* = ∑*C*~j~; *Q* is compared with a χ^2^ distribution with *m* − 1 degrees of freedom

The ML model was then compared with each conventional method by McNemar's test (McNemar, 1947), which uses only the records on which the two differ:

$$χ^2=(|b-c|-1)^2/(b+c)$$ {#eq:mcnemar}

where:
: *b* = records diagnosed correctly by the ML model and wrongly by the conventional method; *c* = the reverse

When *b* + *c* < 25 the exact binomial test was used instead. The three p-values of each test set were adjusted by Holm's procedure (Holm, 1979), which keeps the chance of any false positive at 5 %: the *i*-th smallest p-value is significant while *p*~i~ ≤ 0.05/(*m* − *i* + 1), with *m* = 3. The effect size is the odds ratio *b*/*c* with its 95 % interval.

### Is Disagreement Concentrated near the Boundaries?

For each conventional method, the probability that the ML diagnosis and the method's diagnosis disagree was modelled by logistic regression on the standardised boundary distance:

$$ln(π_i/(1-π_i))=β_0+β_1 d_i,  "OR"=e^(β_1)$$ {#eq:logit}

where:
: *π*~i~ = probability that the ML and the method's diagnoses differ for record *i*
: *d*~i~ = boundary distance of record *i* (@eq:dist-ratio or @eq:dist-duval), standardised to zero mean and unit standard deviation
: *β*~1~ = change of the log-odds of disagreement per standard deviation of distance; *OR* = odds ratio

A negative *β*~1~, with a 95 % interval that excludes zero, means that records closer to a boundary are more often disputed. Records for which a distance cannot be computed were excluded and counted. Firth's penalised likelihood (Firth, 1993) was available in case of separation but was not needed.

### Research Hypothesis and Decision Criteria {#sec:hyp-criteria}

The research hypothesis (@sec:ch1) is called **supported** only if all three of the following criteria hold on the main benchmark, the inspection-confirmed IEC TC 10 power transformers; otherwise it is reported as partly supported or not supported:

1. the ML model has a higher strict accuracy than each conventional method, with a Holm-adjusted McNemar p-value below 0.05;
2. the advantage comes mainly from the records on which a method gives no diagnosis, that is, the gap in conditional accuracy is smaller than the gap in strict accuracy; and
3. disagreement between ML and each method becomes more likely closer to the method's decision boundary (*β*~1~ < 0 with a 95 % interval that excludes zero).

## Reduced-Gas Study

With five gases there are 2^5^ − 1 = 31 non-empty gas subsets. For every subset *S*, the selected pipeline was retrained and evaluated by the same nested cross-validation on the same folds, using only what *S* allows: the logarithm of each gas in *S*, the shares within the gases of *S*, a ratio only when both of its gases are in *S*, and the Duval percentages only when CH4, C2H4 and C2H2 are all present. With all five gases this is exactly the full feature set. The loss of performance is

$$Δ_S="Macro-F1"_"full"-"Macro-F1"_S$$ {#eq:loss}

and the **minimum gas panel** is the subset with the fewest gases whose loss satisfies

$$U_95 (Δ_S )≤δ$$ {#eq:panel}

where:
: *Δ*~S~ = loss of macro-F1 when only the gases in subset *S* are available
: *U*~95~ = upper limit of the 95 % paired bootstrap interval of the loss (*B* = 2 000)
: *δ* = 0.05 = largest acceptable loss, fixed before the study

For the conventional methods, applicability follows from their formulas: the ratio methods need all five gases, and the Duval Triangle needs CH4, C2H4 and C2H2. This was confirmed by running the MATLAB implementations with each removed gas marked as not measured.

## Thermal Model of the Transformer

### Purpose

The Simulink model represents the operating condition of one power transformer, namely its load, oil and winding temperatures and the ageing of its paper insulation, so that a DGA diagnosis can be shown in the context in which the oil sample was taken. Its structure and implementation are described in Chapter 8; this section gives the equations it solves and the way it was verified.

### Thermal Equations

The model implements the differential equations of the IEC 60076-7 loading guide (IEC, 2018). The top-oil temperature follows

$$k_11 τ_o  (dθ_o)/dt=((1+K^2 R)/(1+R))^x Δθ_or-(θ_o-θ_a)$$ {#eq:topoil}

and the hot-spot temperature is the top-oil temperature plus the difference of two first-order components:

$$k_21 Δθ_hr K^y=k_22 τ_w  (dΔθ_h1)/dt+Δθ_h1,  (k_21-1) Δθ_hr K^y=τ_o/k_22  (dΔθ_h2)/dt+Δθ_h2$$ {#eq:hotspot}

$$θ_h=θ_o+Δθ_h1-Δθ_h2,  Δθ_hr=H g_r,  R=P_k/P_0$$ {#eq:theta-h}

where:
: *θ*~o~, *θ*~h~, *θ*~a~ = top-oil, hot-spot and ambient temperature (°C); *t* = time (min)
: *K* = load factor, the load current divided by the rated current (pu); *R* = ratio of load loss to no-load loss
: *Δθ*~or~ = top-oil temperature rise at rated load (K); *g*~r~ = winding-to-oil gradient (K); *H* = hot-spot factor
: *k*~11~, *k*~21~, *k*~22~ = thermal model constants; *τ*~o~, *τ*~w~ = oil and winding time constants (min); *x*, *y* = oil and winding exponents
: *Δθ*~h1~, *Δθ*~h2~ = the two components of the hot-spot-to-top-oil gradient (K); *P*~k~, *P*~0~ = load and no-load losses (W)

### Insulation Ageing

The relative ageing rate of the paper insulation and the loss of life over a period are

$$V=2^((θ_h-98)/6),  L=∫_(t_1)^(t_2) V dt$$ {#eq:ageing}

where:
: *V* = relative ageing rate (1 at a hot-spot temperature of 98 °C; it doubles for every 6 K more)
: *L* = loss of life over the period from *t*~1~ to *t*~2~, expressed in hours of ageing at 98 °C (h)

### Verification Method

The model was verified against an independent numerical solution of @eq:topoil to @eq:theta-h, computed with a stiff differential-equation solver at a tight tolerance and driven by the load factor measured in the model. Two tests were run: rated load from a cold start (ambient 20 °C, 24 h) and a load step from 0.6 to 1.2 pu at 6 h. The measures were

$$e_"max"=max┬t |θ_"sim"(t)-θ_"ref"(t)|,  "RMSE"=√(1/M ∑_(m=1)^M (θ_"sim"(t_m)-θ_"ref"(t_m))^2)$$ {#eq:emax}

where:
: *θ*~sim~, *θ*~ref~ = top-oil or hot-spot temperature from the model and from the reference (°C); *M* = number of compared time points after the first 10 min

The acceptance criteria, approved before the tests, were *e*~max~ ≤ 0.1 K for both temperatures in both tests and a 63 % rise time of the top-oil temperature within 1 min of *k*~11~*τ*~o~. The model was also checked with the Simulink Model Advisor.

## Integration and Evaluation of the Framework

### Diagnosis Service and Dashboard

The released ML model is offered to other programs through a web service: a program sends the five gas concentrations as a JSON message over HTTP and receives the diagnosis and the class probabilities. The service validates every request strictly and refuses invalid input with an explanation, so that a wrong or missing value cannot silently produce a diagnosis. A dashboard built with MATLAB App Designer calls the conventional methods and the service and shows both diagnoses together. Their design is described in Chapter 8.

### Fidelity and Response Time

A deployed system is useful only if it returns exactly the results that were evaluated offline. The integrated system was therefore run over all held-out records, and its fidelity was measured as

$$F=n_"match"/N$$ {#eq:fidelity}

where:
: *F* = fidelity; *n*~match~ = records for which the ML class returned by the system equals the offline prediction; *N* = records sent

The response time is the time between sending a request to the service and receiving the answer, summarised by the median and the 95th percentile. The requirements, fixed in advance, were *F* = 1 and a 95th-percentile response time below 1 000 ms, a limit suitable for interactive use.

## Exploratory Improvement Study

After the main results were known, an exploratory study was carried out to see how the model could be improved. Its protocol was registered before any of its models was trained, and the main results were not changed. Records of the four sources were de-duplicated, and leave-one-source-out validation was used: each source in turn was held out and the models were trained on the others. Three models were compared: A, the released model unchanged; B, the same pipeline retrained on the other sources; and C, a hybrid model that adds the conventional diagnoses, the normality-gate flags and the boundary distances to the inputs of B. A **reject option** (Chow, 1970) was also examined: the hybrid model abstains on its least confident records until its coverage equals that of a ratio method, and its accuracy on the records it answers is compared with that method's conditional accuracy. Because the held-out sources had already been seen in the main study, this study is labelled exploratory.
