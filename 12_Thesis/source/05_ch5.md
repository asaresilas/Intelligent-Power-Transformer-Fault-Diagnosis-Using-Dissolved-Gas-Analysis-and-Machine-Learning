# Diagnostic Performance of the Conventional and Machine-Learning Methods {#sec:ch5}

## Introduction

This chapter reports how well each diagnostic method performs on its own. It first presents the conventional methods (@sec:r-conv), then the comparison of the five classifiers by cross-validation and the choice of the final model (@sec:r-cv and @sec:r-select), the performance of that model on the test sets (@sec:r-test), and the explanation of its decisions (@sec:r-explain). The direct statistical comparison of the ML model with the conventional methods follows in Chapter 6.

## Conventional Methods {#sec:r-conv}

### Verification of the Implementation {#sec:conv-verification}

The MATLAB implementation of the three conventional methods passed all 67 unit tests and reproduced the worked examples of the sources. The independent Python implementation gave the same answer for every method on every one of the {{n_master}} records, 2 481 answers in all, and the boundary distances of the two implementations agreed to within 10^-9^. The conventional answers used in the rest of this thesis can therefore be taken as a faithful application of the published rules.

### Answers over All Records

Over all {{n_master}} records, after the normality gate, the IEC 60599 method gave no diagnosis for {{iec_nd_all}} records ({{iec_nd_all_pct}}) and the Rogers method for {{rogers_nd_all}} records ({{rogers_nd_all_pct}}). The Duval Triangle gave an answer for all but {{duval_nd_all}} record, but {{duval_dt_all}} of its answers fell in the DT zone, which has no matching fault class. The IEC method gave the dual answer D1/D2 for {{iec_dual_all}} records, and the normality gate declared {{gate_upper_all}} records Normal.

### Sensitivity to the Normality Gate

@tab:sensitivity shows how the results change when the gate is moved or removed. Without the gate the conventional methods do better on the IEC TC 10 benchmark: the strict accuracy of the Duval Triangle rises from {{iec_duv_strict}} to {{pm_iec_no_gate_duv_strict}} and that of IEC 60599 from {{iec_iecm_strict}} to {{pm_iec_no_gate_iecm_strict}}. Every benchmark case is a confirmed fault, so a gate that declares some of them Normal can only lower the score; several inspected faults had gas levels within the typical ranges. On the internal test set, which contains Normal records, the gate is needed for the methods to recognise them. The gate with the upper ends of the typical ranges was fixed in advance as the main analysis and is kept as such; the sensitivity results are discussed with the comparison in Chapter 6.

## Comparison of the Classifiers {#sec:r-cv}

### Cross-Validation Results

@tab:cv and @fig:cvbox give the results of the nested cross-validation on the {{n_dev}} development records. Every classifier was far better than the majority baseline (macro-F1 {{cv_F4_majority}}). Three findings stand out:

- **Engineered features help.** For every classifier, the ratio-based feature sets F3 or F4 gave a higher mean macro-F1 than the log-gas concentrations (F1) or the gas shares (F2).
- **Tree ensembles perform best.** The best pipeline was the random forest on F4 (macro-F1 {{cv_F4_random_forest}} ± {{cv_F4_random_forest_sd}}), followed by XGBoost on F4 ({{cv_F4_xgboost}}) and on F3 ({{cv_F3_xgboost}}).
- **The simple model is close behind.** Logistic regression on F3 reached {{cv_F3_logistic_regression}}, less than 0.03 below the best pipeline.

The per-class results (Appendix B) show that D1 and T2 were the hardest classes for every classifier (random forest F1 {{cv_rf_f1_D1}} and {{cv_rf_f1_T2}}), while T3 and Normal were the easiest ({{cv_rf_f1_T3}} and {{cv_rf_f1_Normal}}). The Friedman test showed that the six models on F4 differ in their ranks over the 50 folds (χ^2^ = {{friedman_chi2}}, p {{friedman_p}}). @fig:cd shows the average ranks with the Nemenyi critical difference of {{nemenyi_cd}}. ## Selection of the Final Model {#sec:r-select}

The written selection rule (@sec:ch3) compared the best pipeline, the random forest on F4, with the second best, XGBoost on F4. The corrected resampled t-test gave a mean difference of {{sel_diff}} in macro-F1, t = {{sel_t}}, p = {{sel_p}}. The difference is not significant, and the random forest is the simpler of the two model families, so **the random forest on feature set F4 was selected**. Seven other pipelines were also not significantly worse than the best (same test, without correction for multiple comparisons), among them logistic regression on F3; the advantage of complex models on these data is therefore small. The final model was tuned on all development records, which gave 500 trees without a depth limit and a minimum leaf size of one record, and was saved before any test record was predicted.

## Explaining the Model {#sec:r-explain}

### Which Gases Matter

At the level of whole gases (@fig:perm a), shuffling C2H4 caused the largest drop in macro-F1 on the internal test set ({{pi_int_C2H4}}), followed by C2H2, H2 and C2H6, while CH4 mattered least. On the IEC TC 10 benchmark the order was {{pi_iec_order}}; shuffling C2H6 there even slightly improved the score, which suggests that the way the model uses ethane, learned from the development data, does not transfer well to these cases. At the level of single features (@fig:perm b) the importances are small and their intervals include zero, because the information of each gas is shared by several correlated features (@sec:describe); this is why the gas-level ranking is the main one.

![Permutation importance of the final model on the internal test set (n = 61): (a) whole gases shuffled, (b) single features shuffled; bars show the mean drop over 30 shuffles with its 2.5–97.5 % range](12_Thesis/figures/thesis/permutation_importance.png){#fig:perm width=15.5}

### How the Features Push Each Class

The SHAP summary (@fig:shap) shows how each feature pushes the probability of each class. The model's reasoning agrees with gas-formation physics (@tab:engineering): for each of the six fault classes, at least one of the three most important features involves the key gas that was written down in advance ({{eng_consistent}} classes). High shares of C2H2 push the model towards D1 and D2; a low CH4/H2 ratio and a high hydrogen share push it towards PD; and a high C2H4/C2H6 ratio pushes it towards T2 and T3. The ranking of the features on the test records agreed closely with that on the development records (Spearman correlation {{shap_spearman}}), so the model reasons in the same way on new data.

## Discussion

The conventional methods behave as the literature describes (@sec:ch2): the ratio methods leave many samples without a diagnosis, and the Duval Triangle answers every sample and is the most accurate of the three (Ekojono *et al.*, 2022; Suwarno *et al.*, 2024). Their high conditional accuracy shows that the problem of the ratio methods is mainly coverage, not reliability: when they answer, they are usually right.

The cross-validation macro-F1 of {{cv_F4_random_forest}} is lower than the accuracies above 95 % reported in many studies (Das *et al.*, 2023; Hechifa *et al.*, 2024; Adekunle *et al.*, 2025). Three features of this study explain much of the difference. Macro-F1 over seven classes gives the small and difficult classes D1 and T2 the same weight as the easy ones, whereas accuracy rewards the large classes. Leakage was removed: {{n_removed_leak}} development records that copied test or benchmark cases were deleted and near-duplicates were kept in one fold. Finally, the test sets were predicted only once, after the model had been chosen. Studies that test on independent data or under a strict protocol also report lower figures (Hechifa *et al.*, 2024; Nkwambe and Thango, 2026), in line with the warning of Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) that leakage inflates reported performance.

The selection of a random forest, with XGBoost statistically indistinguishable, agrees with the literature, in which tree ensembles perform best on DGA data (Rao *et al.*, 2021; Adekunle *et al.*, 2025; Doğan *et al.*, 2026). That logistic regression on gas ratios came within 0.03 of the best model is less often reported; it supports the finding of Chen and Zhang (2025) that more complex models add little on DGA data and shows the value of a simple baseline. The benefit of the ratio features agrees with Hechifa *et al.* (2024) and Taha, Ibrahim and Mansour (2021). Finally, the agreement of the explanations with gas-formation physics is evidence that the model has learned genuine fault signatures rather than artefacts of the data, which is a precondition for engineers to trust it.

## Summary

The conventional methods were implemented faithfully, with identical answers from two independent implementations. The IEC 60599 and Rogers methods left {{iec_nd_all_pct}} and {{rogers_nd_all_pct}} of all records without a diagnosis, and the Duval Triangle was the best conventional method. Among the classifiers, the random forest and XGBoost on the full feature set performed best in cross-validation, and the random forest on F4 was selected by the written rule. On the test sets it reached macro-F1 {{int_ml_f1}} (internal), {{iec_ml_f1}} (IEC TC 10) and {{li_ml_f1}} (Li 2019). Its explanations agree with the known key gases of each fault. Chapter 6 now compares the model and the conventional methods statistically on the same records.
