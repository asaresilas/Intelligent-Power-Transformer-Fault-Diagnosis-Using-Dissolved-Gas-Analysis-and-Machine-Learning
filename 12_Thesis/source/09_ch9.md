# Conclusions and Recommendations {#sec:ch9}

## Introduction

This research developed and evaluated an integrated MATLAB/Simulink–Python framework for diagnosing incipient faults of power transformers from measured dissolved gases. The framework produces conventional and machine-learning diagnoses from the same record, compares them statistically, and presents both to the engineer next to the simulated operating condition of the transformer. This chapter states the conclusions for each objective, answers the research questions, summarises the contributions and limitations of the work, and gives recommendations and suggestions for further work.

## Conclusions

The conclusions are given objective by objective:

- **Conventional methods.** The Rogers ratio, IEC 60599 ratio and Duval Triangle 1 methods were implemented in MATLAB from documented tables and verified against the worked examples of their sources and against an independent Python implementation, which gave identical answers on all 2 481 method–record pairs. The ratio methods left a large share of records without a diagnosis (IEC 60599 {{iec_nd_all_pct}} and Rogers {{rogers_nd_all_pct}} of {{n_master}} records), while the Duval Triangle always answered and was the most accurate conventional method on every test set.
- **Data.** A table of {{n_master}} published records with five gases was assembled with documented label origin. The audit found and removed {{n_removed_leak}} development records that leaked test or benchmark cases, and it found that one published training file contains IEC TC 10 cases with relabelled faults. Only the {{n_iec}} inspection-confirmed IEC TC 10 power transformers were therefore used as the main benchmark.
- **Machine learning.** Five classifiers were compared by leakage-free nested cross-validation. A random forest on 18 engineered features was selected (macro-F1 {{cv_F4_random_forest}}); it was statistically indistinguishable from XGBoost, and simple logistic regression came within 0.03. The model's explanations agree with gas-formation physics: acetylene drives the discharge classes, hydrogen and the CH4/H2 ratio drive partial discharge, and ethylene drives the high-temperature thermal faults.
- **Reduced gases.** Only one reduced panel kept the loss of macro-F1 within the tolerance of {{rg_delta}}: H2, C2H6, C2H4 and C2H2, without methane (loss {{rg_min_loss}} {{rg_min_loss_ci}}). No conventional method can interpret this panel, because all of them need methane. Hydrogen alone was far from sufficient for diagnosis.
- **ML versus conventional methods.** On the internal and Li test sets the ML model was significantly more accurate than every conventional method. On the inspection-confirmed IEC TC 10 benchmark it was significantly more accurate than the Rogers method only; it did not differ significantly from IEC 60599, and the Duval Triangle had the higher point estimate ({{iec_duv_strict}} against {{iec_ml_strict}}). ML and the conventional methods agreed only fairly to moderately. With the normality gate, disagreement was not concentrated near the decision boundaries; without it, disagreement with the Duval Triangle did increase near its zone boundaries.
- **Integration.** A Simulink model of a 40 MVA transformer met its verification tolerance by a wide margin (largest hot-spot difference {{sim_t2_emax_hs}} K against 0.1 K). A FastAPI service and a MATLAB App Designer dashboard were linked to it. Over all {{n_heldout}} held-out records the integrated system returned exactly the offline diagnoses, with a 95th-percentile response time of {{int_p95}} ms against the 1 000 ms target.

The research hypothesis, that a leakage-free ML classifier would diagnose more samples correctly than every conventional method, mainly because it answers where they do not, with disagreement concentrated near their boundaries, was **not supported** on the main benchmark and **partly supported** on the other two test sets.

The overall conclusion is that machine learning is a useful complement to conventional DGA interpretation, not a replacement for it. On clearly inspected faults the best conventional method is as accurate as a carefully validated ML model. ML, however, always gives an answer, works with gas panels that the conventional methods cannot use, and disagrees with them often enough that showing both answers together gives the engineer real information. The exploratory study suggests that a model that answers only when it is confident could be more accurate than the ratio methods at the same coverage; this needs confirmation on fresh data.

## Contributions of the Research

The research makes the following contributions:

- a provenance-audited DGA data table in which the origin of every fault label is documented and leakage between training and test data has been removed, with evidence that a widely available training file contains relabelled benchmark cases;
- a comparison of ML with three conventional methods on the same inspection-confirmed records, using paired tests, coverage-aware accuracy and the home-advantage caveat, with the hypothesis criteria fixed before the results were computed;
- a systematic study of all 31 gas subsets, identifying a four-gas panel that keeps the ML performance but cannot be interpreted by any conventional method;
- an analysis of where ML and conventional diagnoses disagree, relating disagreement to the distance from the conventional decision boundaries; and
- a working, verified framework that shows conventional and ML diagnoses side by side on a simulated transformer and reproduces the offline results exactly.

## Limitations of the Study

The conclusions are limited by the following:

- Only the IEC TC 10 data have inspection-confirmed labels, and among its 58 power transformers there is only one PD case and eight T1/T2 cases, so per-class results on the benchmark carry wide intervals. The labels of the other data sets are of unknown origin.
- The IEC 60599 limits were refined on the IEC TC 10 cases, giving the conventional methods a home advantage on the main benchmark.
- Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide, which indicate faults involving paper, could not be used because most records lack them.
- The full texts of IEC 60599:2022, IEEE C57.104-2019 and IEC 60076-7:2018 were not available. The limits come from the official previews of IEC 60599, the 2008 IEEE guide and peer-reviewed secondary sources.
- The Simulink model represents a typical 40 MVA transformer with default thermal constants and illustrative load scenarios, and the framework was tested on a single laptop computer.
- The improvement study is exploratory and needs confirmation on fresh data.

## Recommendations

The following are recommended:

- Utilities that interpret DGA results should show conventional and ML diagnoses side by side, as the dashboard of this study does, and treat samples on which they disagree as candidates for closer surveillance or resampling, rather than replacing the conventional methods.
- The Duval Triangle should be kept as the reference conventional method. The Rogers ratio method should not be used alone, because it cannot diagnose low-energy discharges and leaves many samples without a diagnosis.
- When online monitors are chosen for fault diagnosis rather than detection, monitors that measure at least H2, C2H6, C2H4 and C2H2 should be preferred, and hydrogen-only monitors should be treated as fault detectors, not as diagnostic tools.
- Researchers who report ML results for DGA should document the origin of their labels, remove duplicate and leaked records, use nested cross-validation and a class-balanced measure such as macro-F1, test on inspection-confirmed cases, compare with the conventional methods by paired tests, and state the home advantage of the conventional methods on the IEC TC 10 database.
- Ghanaian utilities, such as the Ghana Grid Company, the Electricity Company of Ghana and the Volta River Authority, should be approached through the University for DGA records with inspection-confirmed faults; such data are needed to confirm the findings on local transformers.

## Suggestions for Further Work

Further work could:

- validate the model and the reject option on new inspection-confirmed data, preferably from Ghanaian utilities, and extend the inputs to CO and CO2 so that faults involving paper can be identified;
- replace the default thermal constants and the illustrative scenarios of the Simulink model with the nameplate and heat-run data and the measured load profiles of a real transformer, and confirm the limits of the conventional methods against the current full editions of IEC 60599, IEEE C57.104 and IEC 60076-7;
- add the trend of gas generation rates and the uncertainty of laboratory measurements to the diagnosis; and
- harden the service for networked use, with authentication, logging and monitoring, and test it on utility hardware.
